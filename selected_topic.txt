DDoS Protection
defending against Distributed Denial of Service attacks that overwhelm services with traffic; volumetric attacks vs application-layer attacks; network-level scrubbing (Cloudflare, AWS Shield); rate limiting and connection limits as application-layer defences; the detection problem (legitimate traffic spike vs attack). Angle note: distinguishing a DDoS from a successful product launch is harder than it sounds — the detection and response tooling is where the interesting engineering lives.

Hook: On a Saturday in December 2025, a botnet called Aisuru threw 31.4 terabits of traffic per second at a target — enough to overwhelm the entire internet infrastructure of some countries — and the attack was over in 35 seconds. Fourteen months earlier, the record had been 3.8 Tbps. Scrubbing centers today can absorb that. What they still struggle to do, in real time, is tell it apart from a website's best day ever.
Angle: The story of DDoS protection isn't really an arms race of raw capacity (that problem is largely solved by distributed anycast scrubbing) — it's a detection problem that's stayed stubbornly hard for two decades: at the packet level, a flash crowd and a flood attack can look identical, and the entire discipline of modern mitigation is about reading the rhythm of a crowd to tell genuine enthusiasm from a coordinated mob.
Key facts:
- DDoS record size has exploded recently: 3.8 Tbps (Oct 2024) to 22.2 Tbps to 31.4 Tbps (Dec 2025, the Aisuru IoT botnet, ~1-4 million infected devices), yet the record attack lasted only 35 seconds — modern DDoS is often a short, saturating burst rather than a siege.
- The 2016 Mirai/Dyn attack (1 Tbps, took down Twitter, Netflix, PayPal, Reddit, GitHub) was a genuine turning point: it came right after the Mirai source code was leaked publicly, and it proved that ordinary consumer IoT devices (routers, cameras, DVRs) with unchanged default passwords could be conscripted into an army bigger than any botnet before it.
- Scrubbing works via anycast + BGP: many geographically spread data centers announce the same IP, so an attack that would flatten one location gets split dozens of ways automatically; traffic is cleaned and sent back over a GRE tunnel while outbound replies skip the detour, halving the latency cost compared to routing both directions through the scrubber.
- Researchers still can't cleanly automate the difference between a "flash crowd" (a link going viral, a product launch) and a DDoS attack using traffic volume alone — both are legitimate-looking simultaneous surges; the tell is in finer-grained behavioral signals like packet arrival rhythm and traffic entropy, not raw numbers.
Analogy: A doorman at a restaurant that suddenly goes viral overnight versus one facing a flash mob sent to block the entrance — from the street, both look like an instant crowd surge; only by watching how people move (do they arrive in the same rhythm, do they behave like distinct individuals, do they ever actually get seated) can he tell a real full house from a staged siege.
