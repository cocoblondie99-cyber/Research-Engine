Somatic experiencing and body-based trauma therapy (CEN)
in the context of CEN recovery: Peter Levine's approach and why a body-based method specifically suits neglect (where the wound is the absence of felt safety rather than a discrete event); titration, pendulation, and the felt sense as tools; how tracking sensation in the body differs from talking about feelings; what somatic work addresses that cognitive approaches often miss in CEN cases

ANGLE AND HOOK PLAN:
Open with the puzzle at the heart of CEN therapy: talk therapy wants a story, but neglect left no story — only an absence, and you cannot narrate an absence. The hook is this mismatch: the primary modality of psychological healing (talking) presupposes something happened, while the entire signature of neglect is that nothing did. From there, build through Levine's founding observation — that animals discharge trauma through shaking and trembling after a threat, while humans, socialised out of this, hold survival energy frozen in the body — and use this as the lens for titration (approaching traumatic activation in tiny doses so the nervous system can process without flooding) and pendulation (oscillating between activation and settled safety to expand tolerance). The piece's emotional core is alexithymia: many CEN survivors literally cannot feel their feelings because interoception — the body's sense of its own internal state — was never developed or named; somatic work is therefore not an alternative route to the same destination as CBT but access to a prior, pre-verbal layer that CBT cannot reach. Close on what changes: the goal is not catharsis or insight but something quieter — a felt sense of occupying one's own body as a safe place, perhaps for the first time.
