Cron Jobs

Time-based task scheduling using cron expressions; the anatomy of a cron expression; what cron jobs are suited for (recurring batch work) and where they fail (distributed environments, missing-run detection, overlapping executions); how Kubernetes CronJobs handle some of these problems; alternatives for high-reliability scheduling. Angle note: cron's footguns in distributed systems (multiple instances, no locking, silent failures) are the story — what looks simple in a single-server world becomes a source of subtle bugs at scale.

Hook: In 1977, Unix engineers built a small program to wake up once a minute, glance at a list of jobs, and run whatever was due — they called it cron, after the Greek word for time, and fifty years later it is still quietly running more of the internet's invisible plumbing (backups, log rotation, billing runs, health checks) than almost any other piece of software you've never heard of.
Angle: Cron looks like a solved problem — five fields, a table, a daemon — but its very simplicity is what makes it dangerous once you leave a single machine. The piece traces cron from Ken Thompson's one-minute wakeup loop to Paul Vixie's per-user crontabs to Kubernetes CronJobs, using that arc to show a recurring pattern in engineering: a design assumption (one server, one clock, one list) that works perfectly until scale quietly breaks it, and the elaborate machinery (distributed locks, concurrency policies, missed-schedule limits) later generations must bolt on to recover the guarantees the original design gave away for free.
Key facts:
- Cron's core algorithm has barely changed since Ken Thompson wrote it for Unix in the early 1970s (formalized in Version 7 Unix, 1977): a daemon wakes up every minute, reads a table, and fires anything scheduled for that minute.
- The five-field format (minute, hour, day-of-month, month, day-of-week) and per-user crontabs were introduced by Paul Vixie in the 1980s and later standardized in POSIX (1992) — the same syntax now drives everything from Linux servers to GitHub Actions.
- When cron's single-server assumption is stretched across a distributed system, jobs silently double-run — Kubernetes CronJobs need explicit concurrencyPolicy settings (Allow/Forbid/Replace) and a startingDeadlineSeconds cutoff just to restore behavior a single crontab gave for free; without equivalent locking, overlapping runs "rarely fail loudly," they fail by slowly corrupting data.
Analogy: cron as an old lighthouse keeper's routine — a solitary, reliable ritual (check the light every hour, on the hour) that works beautifully for one keeper on one rock, but turns to chaos the moment you put five keepers on five rocks all trying to follow the same instruction sheet without talking to each other; the modern fixes (locks, leader election, deadlines) are essentially inventing a radio so the keepers can coordinate.
