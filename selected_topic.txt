Connection Pooling

Connection Pooling — reusing database connections across requests rather than opening and closing a connection per query; why connection setup is expensive (TLS handshake, auth, network round trips); pool sizing and the tradeoff between too few (queuing) and too many (overloading the database); PgBouncer as a connection pooler; the pool as a throttle as well as a cache. Angle note: connection pooling is one of those optimisations that goes from optional to mandatory as traffic grows — quantify the cost of not having it.

Hook: A single database query that takes 2 milliseconds to run can sit behind 80 milliseconds of setup — a TCP handshake, a TLS negotiation, and an authentication check — meaning the "work" is sometimes less than 3% of the time spent. Connection pooling is the fix nobody notices until it's missing, at which point it's the only thing that matters.
Angle: Frame pooling through its two faces at once — it's a cache (reusing expensive-to-build connections instead of remaking them) and a throttle (capping how many can exist so the database doesn't drown). Most explanations only cover the cache half; the throttle half is what makes pool *sizing* a real engineering decision rather than "bigger is better." Trace the arc: why a fresh connection is expensive, how a pool amortises that cost, then why an oversized pool becomes its own outage (PostgreSQL's max_connections crashes are almost always a pooling failure, not a traffic failure) — and where PgBouncer's transaction-mode multiplexing fits as the production answer.
Key facts:
- Opening a fresh PostgreSQL connection costs roughly 10-40ms (TCP handshake ~1-3ms, TLS ~5-30ms, auth ~2-5ms) before a single query runs; in one measured example this setup was 93% of total request time.
- Each open connection isn't free to sit idle either — every PostgreSQL backend is a full OS process holding a few MB of RAM plus working memory, so cranking max_connections up to "solve" exhaustion errors can crash the server via memory pressure or swap, which is why the real fix is a pooler like PgBouncer sitting in front, not a bigger number.
- Pool sizing has a counter-intuitive answer: HikariCP's widely-cited formula is (CPU cores × 2) + disk spindles — often just 10-20 connections total — because past the point where cores can actually execute queries in parallel, more connections just add context-switching and lock-contention overhead rather than more throughput.
Analogy: A connection pool is an airport's rental-car fleet rather than a lot where every traveller shows up having built their own car from scratch (registering it, insuring it, fuelling it — the equivalent of a fresh handshake, auth, and TLS setup every time). The fleet sits ready at the curb; you check a car out, drive, and return it for the next traveller. Fleet size is the whole game: too few cars and travellers queue at the counter (latency, the "too small" pool problem); too many cars and the airport's own roads and parking gridlock under the weight of vehicles it's trying to serve (the "too large" pool problem, mirrored by max_connections exhaustion crashes). PgBouncer's transaction-pooling mode is the fleet manager who realised most travellers only need the car for a quick errand, not the whole day — so the same small fleet can serve far more people than a naive one-traveller-one-car system ever could.
