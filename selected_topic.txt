Leader Election
**35. Leader Election** — how distributed systems choose a single coordinator node to make decisions; why you need one (split-brain, conflicting writes); Raft and Paxos as the consensus algorithms; ZooKeeper and etcd as the practical tools; the epoch/fencing token pattern for preventing stale leaders from causing damage. Angle note: the split-brain scenario — two nodes both believe they're the leader — is the failure mode that makes this problem viscerally important.

Hook: In 2018, GitHub's ZooKeeper cluster briefly had two leaders at once during routine maintenance — writes to Kafka started failing, and the outage cascaded from there. Two machines, each certain it alone was in charge.
Angle: Frame leader election as a courtroom problem — not "how do computers pick a boss" but "how do you get strangers who can't fully trust each other, or the network between them, to agree on one truth without a referee." The piece follows the escalation: naive approaches fail (why can't you just have one machine be leader?), quorum-based consensus (Raft) fixes agreement, but even a correctly-elected leader can go stale — so the final safeguard is fencing tokens, which don't prevent a second leader from existing but prevent it from doing damage.
Key facts:
- Split-brain isn't a bug in the election algorithm — it's what happens when a network partition makes both halves of a cluster behave *correctly* according to their own local view, simultaneously. GitHub's 2018 ZooKeeper incident happened because new nodes were added too fast during an upgrade and couldn't agree on cluster topology, triggering a leader election that produced two leaders.
- Raft solves election by majority quorum: a candidate needs votes from over half the cluster, and randomized election timeouts (e.g., 150-300ms) prevent repeated tied elections. This is why production clusters (etcd, used by Kubernetes) always run odd numbers of nodes — 3 or 5 — so a majority is always mathematically possible.
- Even a legitimately-elected leader can go "zombie": Martin Kleppmann's insight is that a leader can be paused (GC, network delay) for long enough that its lease expires and a new leader is elected — but the old one wakes up unaware and keeps acting like leader. The fix isn't preventing this (provably impossible to fully prevent) but fencing tokens: a strictly increasing number attached to every leader action, so the resource being written to can reject stale writes from an expired leader even if that leader doesn't know it's expired.
Analogy: A courtroom/jury framing — election is like a jury reaching a verdict by majority rather than requiring unanimous consent (any one absent/silent juror can't block a decision), and fencing tokens are like a judge date-stamping every ruling so a courtroom clerk can always tell which ruling is the most recent and throw out an outdated order — even if the old judge doesn't realize they've been replaced.
