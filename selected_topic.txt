Health Checks

**48. Health Checks** — endpoints or probes that report whether a service instance is healthy and ready to serve traffic; HTTP /health endpoints as the convention; what to check (dependency availability, resource levels) vs what to skip; the difference between being "alive" and being "ready"; health checks as the foundation of load balancer and orchestrator decisions. Angle note: health checks are the service's honest answer to "can you handle requests right now?" — the interesting design question is what honest means.

Hook: In February 2026 a real outage story: an engineer wires a health check to ping the database directly — one 200 OK meant "everything's fine." One slow query later, every instance in the fleet reported itself unhealthy at once, got restarted simultaneously, and the herd of restarts hit the database at the same moment, taking the whole system down. The health check meant to prevent an outage caused one.
Angle: A health check looks like a simple yes/no gate, but it's actually a small, opinionated theory of what "okay" means — and that theory has to distinguish between two very different questions: "am I alive" (should you murder and restart me) versus "am I ready" (should you send me customers right now). Conflating them is the single most common way teams accidentally build a self-inflicted outage machine. The piece uses the doctor's-triage-tag analogy to walk from the naive "ping it and see" version through to the modern liveness/readiness/startup split, and lands on the design question the topic note flags: what does "honest" mean when the honest answer is "I'm fine but my database isn't"?
Key facts:
- Liveness and readiness answer different questions and must be checked differently: liveness should be shallow and dependency-free (catches deadlocks/hangs, triggers a restart), while readiness can be "deep" — it's allowed to check whether the database/cache/queue is reachable — because failing readiness only pulls the instance out of traffic rotation rather than killing it. Mixing the two (making liveness depend on downstream services) is the classic mistake, and it manufactures cascading failures: a hiccuping database can cause every service upstream to report itself "dead," triggering mass restarts that then hammer the recovering database even harder.
- Kubernetes added a third probe type, the startup probe, specifically because slow-booting applications kept getting killed by liveness checks before they'd even finished initializing — it exists to draw a line between "still starting" and "started but broken," giving late bloomers a grace period without weakening the liveness check for everyone else.
- There are two failure modes at the extremes of health-check design: the always-200 endpoint that just confirms the process is running (hides real problems, gives false confidence) and the deep-check endpoint that verifies every downstream dependency on every request (creates exactly the cascading-outage risk above, especially when service A's check calls service B's check calls service C's check and one slow link times out the whole chain).
Analogy: A hospital triage tag. "Alive" is the pulse check — crude, fast, dependency-free, and if it fails someone acts immediately (the restart). "Ready" is the fuller assessment — can this patient actually handle being sent into surgery right now, given everything else going on — and a "not ready" doesn't mean they're dying, it just means don't route the next case to them yet. Confusing the two in a hospital would mean sending a patient to the OR because they have a pulse, or declaring them dead because they're not ready for surgery — which is exactly the kind of category error the outage story is built on.
