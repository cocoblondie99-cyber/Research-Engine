Thirty-Five Seconds

On a Saturday morning in December 2025, a botnet called Aisuru aimed 31.4 terabits of traffic per second at a single target. For scale: that's more data every second than the entire internet infrastructure of some mid-sized countries can carry at once, arriving all at the same address, all at the same moment. The attack lasted thirty-five seconds. Fourteen months earlier, the previous record had stood at 3.8 terabits per second — meaning the ceiling on what a botnet could throw at the internet had gone up by a factor of eight in a little over a year. And yet the scrubbing centers built to absorb attacks like this one did their job. The target stayed up. The thing they still can't reliably do, in the first seconds of an event like that, is answer a much simpler-sounding question: is this an attack, or is this just a very, very good day?

That question — attack or enthusiasm? — turns out to be the actual center of gravity in DDoS protection. Not the raw firepower, which is a solved problem in a way that would have seemed impossible a decade ago. The interesting engineering, the part that's stayed stubbornly hard for twenty years running, is telling a coordinated flood apart from a crowd that showed up for entirely legitimate reasons.

**What a DDoS attack actually is**

Strip away the acronym and a Distributed Denial of Service attack is a very old idea wearing new infrastructure: you can disable something not by breaking it, but by making so many simultaneous, legitimate-looking demands on it that it can't serve anyone, including the people it's actually meant to serve. A single overloaded phone line is a denial of service. A DDoS attack is the same principle run at internet scale, with the "distributed" part doing the heavy lifting — instead of one caller redialing over and over, it's thousands or millions of separate devices, each making requests that look, individually, like something an ordinary user might do.

Those devices are usually not the attacker's own hardware. They're a botnet: a sprawling, often unwitting army of compromised machines, recruited one infection at a time and then commanded in unison from wherever the attacker happens to be sitting. The 2016 Mirai botnet is the case study everyone in the field still cites, because it marked the moment the threat model changed. Mirai didn't infect powerful servers — it infected home routers, security cameras, and DVRs, the sort of consumer IoT device that ships with a default admin password nobody ever bothers to change. Its source code leaked publicly that same year, and within weeks a Mirai-powered attack hit Dyn, a company that provided DNS services for a huge slice of the internet. Twitter, Netflix, PayPal, Reddit, GitHub, and dozens of other major sites went dark for hours, not because any of them had been individually hacked, but because the infrastructure translating their names into addresses had been buried under roughly a terabit per second of junk traffic from consumer electronics nobody thought of as a weapon. It was the first time an army built from toasters and baby monitors — objects sitting in millions of ordinary living rooms — outgunned anything a dedicated server farm had thrown before it.

That lineage matters because it explains where the raw capacity keeps coming from. Every year, more devices get connected to the internet with weak or default credentials, and every year the pool of potential foot soldiers gets larger. Aisuru, the botnet behind the December 2025 record, is estimated to have conscripted somewhere between one and four million IoT devices. The scaling problem on the attacker's side isn't technical sophistication — it's inventory. There are simply more insecure smart devices in the world every single year, and each one is a small, disposable contributor to whatever flood someone decides to launch.

**Two very different kinds of flood**

Not all DDoS attacks work the same way, and the distinction matters because the defenses look almost nothing alike.

A volumetric attack is the brute-force version — the kind that produces headline numbers like 31.4 Tbps. The goal is simply to saturate the pipe: throw so much raw data at a target's network connection that legitimate traffic physically cannot get through, the way a driveway can be blocked by enough gravel dumped on it regardless of how well-organized the gravel is. These attacks are often amplified: an attacker sends a small request to a third-party server — a DNS resolver, an NTP time server — using a spoofed source address that makes it look like the request came from the victim. The third-party server, doing exactly what it's supposed to do, sends a much larger response to that spoofed address. A modest request becomes a disproportionate reply, and the attacker gets to multiply their firepower using infrastructure they don't even control.

An application-layer attack is quieter and far harder to spot, because it doesn't try to overwhelm the pipe at all — it targets the expensive part of the software running at the other end. Loading a search results page, running a database query, generating a PDF: these all cost the server real computational work, far more than the trickle of bytes it took to request them. An attacker who sends a modest, entirely legal-looking stream of these expensive requests can exhaust a server's CPU or database connections without ever generating enough raw traffic to look unusual on a network graph. This is the DDoS equivalent of jamming a photocopier not by throwing paper at it, but by feeding it the single most complex document in the building, over and over, until it seizes up — from the outside, nothing about the volume looks wrong at all.

**How the scrubbing actually works**

The defense against the volumetric flood is, structurally, a very elegant piece of engineering, and it rests on two ideas: anycast and BGP.

Ordinarily, an IP address points to one physical place. Anycast breaks that assumption on purpose — the same IP address is announced simultaneously from dozens of data centers scattered around the world, and the internet's routing protocol, BGP, simply sends each visitor's traffic to whichever announcing location is "closest" by network path. A user in Singapore and a user in Frankfurt, typing the identical address into their browser, end up talking to two entirely different buildings without ever knowing it.

This is precisely the shape of defense a volumetric attack needs, because it means an attack aimed at "the" target actually lands on dozens of targets, each absorbing a slice. A flood large enough to flatten a single data center gets divided by the number of scrubbing locations announcing that address, and each site only has to survive its own fraction. Inside each scrubbing center, the incoming traffic gets filtered against a library of known attack signatures and behavioral rules, the junk gets discarded, and the traffic that survives — the legitimate part — gets forwarded on to the actual origin server through an encrypted tunnel. The clever part is that the return trip skips the scrubbing detour entirely: replies go straight back to the user by the normal route, because there's no need to clean traffic flowing away from an attacker. Routing only the inbound leg through the scrubber, instead of both directions, roughly halves the latency penalty a defended site would otherwise pay for being defended at all.

This is genuinely powerful, and it's also why the raw-capacity arms race has mostly been won by the defenders. A 31-terabit attack sounds apocalyptic until you remember it's being divided across dozens of geographically spread scrubbing centers, each one built by a company like Cloudflare or AWS Shield specifically to be larger than anything currently being thrown at the internet. The infrastructure exists now to absorb attacks that would have taken down entire countries' internet connections a decade ago, and it does so almost invisibly, with the target often never noticing anything happened.

**The problem that doesn't go away with more capacity**

None of that solves the harder problem, which is the one Aisuru's thirty-five seconds put in sharp relief: knowing, in real time, what you're even looking at.

Here's the uncomfortable truth at the center of the discipline — a flash crowd and a flood attack can be genuinely indistinguishable using traffic volume alone. A product goes viral. A ticket sale opens. A celebrity mentions a website on live television and a million people click the same link within ninety seconds. From a network's point of view, that surge and a coordinated attack surge can arrive looking like the same shape on the same graph: a sudden, massive, simultaneous spike in requests to the same resource. Researchers have been trying to automate a clean separation between the two for roughly twenty years, and volume-based detection alone still can't do it reliably, because volume is the one variable both scenarios share by definition.

Think about it the way a river authority has to. A town downstream of a dam gets flood warnings from watching water levels rise. But a genuine spring thaw and someone deliberately opening the dam's floodgates both raise the water level — sometimes by comparable amounts, sometimes at comparable speed. If the authority only had a single gauge measuring "how much water is there right now," the two events would be impossible to tell apart until the damage was already done. What actually lets them distinguish a storm from sabotage is everything the level gauge misses: a real thaw rises gradually, over hours, following the rhythm of temperature and rainfall; a deliberate release arrives as a single abrupt wall of water, all at once, from one direction, with none of the gradual buildup a natural event would show. The difference isn't in how much water there is. It's in how it behaves on the way in.

DDoS detection has converged on the same insight. The signal isn't raw volume — it's rhythm and texture. Legitimate flash-crowd traffic tends to arrive with the irregularity of real individual people: browsers negotiating full TLS handshakes, session cookies persisting across multiple page requests, geographic distribution that roughly matches where a company's actual customers live, request patterns that vary the way genuine human behavior varies. Attack traffic, even when it's engineered to mimic legitimate requests, tends to betray itself through statistical uniformity — packets arriving with suspiciously even spacing, a flood of connections that never progress past the first handshake step, requests concentrated on a narrow, oddly specific slice of a site's functionality instead of spread across it the way real visitors browse. Security researchers describe this using a concept borrowed from information theory called entropy: real traffic is messy and high-entropy, full of small variations that don't repeat; coordinated traffic, generated by scripts running the same instructions across a botnet, tends to be lower-entropy than it looks at first glance, even when the attacker has gone to some trouble to disguise it.

This is also where rate limiting and connection limits do their work — not as a wall that blocks everything above a threshold, but as a set of behavioral tripwires. A rate limiter doesn't ask "is this traffic volume alarming," it asks something closer to "is this specific client behaving like a plausible individual user," capping how many requests any single source can make in a given window, or how many connections it's allowed to leave half-open at once. It's a much finer-grained tool than the scrubbing center's flood defense, aimed squarely at the application-layer attacks that never generate enough raw volume to trip a volumetric alarm in the first place.

**Why the detection problem resists a permanent fix**

It's tempting to assume this gets solved eventually — that better machine learning, more historical data, and faster processing will eventually let a system tell attack from enthusiasm with total confidence, the way spam filters mostly got good enough to disappear from daily awareness. But DDoS detection has a structural disadvantage spam filtering doesn't: the traffic being classified isn't static text sitting in an inbox to be analyzed at leisure. It's live, and the cost of guessing wrong runs in both directions. Classify a real flash crowd as an attack and you throttle away paying customers during the exact moment a business most needed the extra capacity to matter. Classify a real attack as a flash crowd and you let a flood through the door while your system tries to figure out whether the doubt is justified.

And attackers adapt specifically to whatever the last generation of detection learned to notice. Once defenders got reasonably good at flagging traffic that was too uniform, attackers started deliberately injecting randomness — varied timing, varied user-agent strings, traffic routed through real residential IP addresses via compromised home devices, so that a botnet request looks, at the level any automated system can currently check, exactly like someone's actual laptop making an actual request from an actual home network. The IoT botnet lineage that started with Mirai's toasters and baby monitors hasn't gone away; it's become the raw material for attacks that are engineered specifically to defeat behavioral detection, not just to add raw volume.

That's the shape of the arms race that's actually still running. Not "how big can an attack get" — that question, oddly, has more or less been answered, since scrubbing infrastructure keeps building headroom ahead of whatever record currently stands. The live question is "how do you tell, in the first few seconds, what kind of surge you're looking at," and every improvement in detection produces a corresponding improvement in disguise. It's less an arms race over firepower than a long-running argument about the nature of a crowd — and crowds, real and manufactured alike, keep getting better at looking like each other.

There's something almost humbling in that. We built systems that can absorb more raw data per second than most nations' internet connections can carry, engineered defenses so effective that a 31-terabit assault becomes a non-event lasting half a minute — and the thing that still trips up the smartest infrastructure on the planet is the same problem a bouncer has always had at a door: not whether there are a lot of people out there, but whether they actually mean it.
