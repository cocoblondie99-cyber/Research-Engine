Three Percent of the Work

A database query that takes 2 milliseconds to run can sit behind 80 milliseconds of setup before it even starts. A TCP handshake. A TLS negotiation. An authentication check. By the time the query itself executes, the "work" — the part anyone actually cares about, the part that fetches the row or updates the balance — is sometimes less than 3% of the total time the request took. The other 97% is a database and a client politely introducing themselves to each other, every single time, as if they'd never met before.

That's the part almost nobody thinks about, because almost nobody has to. Most applications are built on frameworks and libraries that quietly solve this problem before a developer ever notices it exists — a mechanism called connection pooling, sitting a layer or two beneath the code anyone actually writes. It's the kind of infrastructure that succeeds by disappearing. You only find out it was doing something the day it stops.

To see why it matters, it helps to understand what a database connection actually is, and why making a fresh one is so much more expensive than it sounds.

**What a connection costs**

When a web application wants to talk to a database — say, PostgreSQL, one of the most widely used databases in the world — it doesn't just fire off a query into the void. It first has to establish a connection: a stateful, ongoing relationship between the client (your application) and the server (the database), over which queries can then travel back and forth. Setting that relationship up involves several steps, each with its own cost.

First comes the TCP handshake, the basic "hello, can you hear me, yes I can hear you" exchange that underlies almost all network communication — typically 1 to 3 milliseconds, assuming client and server are reasonably close to each other on the network. Then, if the connection is encrypted (and in anything resembling a production system, it should be), there's a TLS negotiation: client and server agree on an encryption method, exchange cryptographic keys, and verify each other's identity, a process that runs anywhere from 5 to 30 milliseconds depending on the handshake type and network latency. Finally there's authentication — the database checking the credentials being presented and deciding whether this client is allowed to do anything at all, another 2 to 5 milliseconds.

Add it up and a fresh PostgreSQL connection typically costs somewhere between 10 and 40 milliseconds before a single row has been read or written. That might sound trivial in isolation. It stops sounding trivial the moment you remember how web applications actually behave: a single page load might trigger a dozen separate database queries, and a busy application might be serving hundreds or thousands of requests per second. If every query opened its own fresh connection, did its 2 milliseconds of actual work, and then closed the connection again, the application would be spending the overwhelming majority of its time and resources on introductions rather than on the thing the database was built to do.

There's a second cost, less visible but just as real: an open connection isn't free to simply exist, either. In PostgreSQL specifically, every connection corresponds to an entire operating-system process on the server, holding a few megabytes of memory just to sit there, plus more working memory once it starts actually doing something. A database with a few hundred open connections is a database quietly setting aside a meaningful chunk of its RAM just to keep those connections alive — memory that isn't available for the caching and query-processing work the database actually exists to do.

So there are two problems stacked on top of each other. Making new connections is slow. And keeping old ones around, once made, is not free either. Connection pooling is the answer to both.

**A fleet, not a car lot**

The idea behind a connection pool is almost embarrassingly simple once you see it: instead of building a new connection every time you need one and discarding it afterward, keep a small number of connections open in advance, hand them out to whoever needs one, and take them back when the work is done. The expensive part — the handshake, the TLS negotiation, the authentication — happens once per connection, not once per query. After that, connections get reused, over and over, by request after request.

Picture an airport rental-car counter. The bad version of this system is one where every traveller who wants a car has to build one from scratch — register it, insure it, fuel it up — before they can drive anywhere. That's what a fresh database connection without pooling looks like: the equivalent bureaucracy repeated by every single traveller, every single time, even though nearly all of them need the car for the same short trip.

The rental-car company obviously doesn't work that way. It keeps a fleet of cars sitting ready at the curb, already registered, insured, and fuelled. A traveller checks one out, drives where they need to go, and returns it. The next traveller gets that same car, not a new one. All the expensive setup happened once, when the fleet was assembled, and gets amortised across everyone who uses it afterward. A connection pool is that fleet: a set of already-authenticated, already-negotiated database connections, sitting ready, waiting to be checked out by whichever part of the application needs to run a query, and returned the moment it's done.

This is the half of connection pooling that gets explained most often, and it's genuinely the more intuitive half: pooling as a cache. Instead of rebuilding something expensive from scratch, you build it once and reuse it. But it's only half the story, and the half that gets left out is the half that makes pool management an actual engineering discipline rather than a box you tick and forget.

**The fleet has to have a size**

Here's the question the cache framing doesn't answer: how many cars should be in the fleet?

The naive instinct is "as many as possible" — surely more available connections can only help, the same way a bigger rental fleet can only serve more travellers. This turns out to be badly wrong, and the reason why is the second, less-discussed job a connection pool does: it isn't just a cache, it's also a throttle. It doesn't just make connections cheaper to reuse — it deliberately limits how many can exist at once, and that limit is what keeps the database from being overwhelmed by its own popularity.

Go back to the airport analogy for a second, but push it further. A fleet that's too small creates an obvious, visible problem: travellers queue at the counter, waiting for a car to come back before they can leave. In database terms, this is what a too-small pool looks like — application threads sitting idle, waiting for a connection to free up, adding latency to every request stuck in that queue. It's an annoying problem, but a legible one; it shows up as slow response times, and the fix is straightforward (make the pool bigger).

A fleet that's too large creates a much less obvious problem, and it's the one people don't see coming: too many cars on the road at once doesn't help the airport move more travellers, it just clogs the airport's own access roads and parking structure, the infrastructure the fleet itself depends on to function. This is the failure mode that catches teams off guard in production. Every open database connection consumes memory and, more importantly, competes for the database's finite CPU cores and locks. Past a certain point, adding more connections doesn't add more throughput — it adds more contention. The database spends more of its time switching between connections and negotiating locks between them, and less time actually running queries. Worse, PostgreSQL (like most databases) has a hard ceiling on how many connections it will accept at all, a setting called max_connections. Teams that hit "too many connections" errors under load often respond by cranking that number up — which is exactly the wrong move, because it invites more of those expensive OS-process, few-megabytes-each connections to pile up, and the database can crash under the resulting memory pressure well before it crashes from the traffic itself. The database, in other words, doesn't die from being too popular. It dies from having too many idle cars parked on its own runway.

This is the counter-intuitive part worth sitting with: past a certain size, a bigger pool doesn't make an application faster. It makes it slower, and eventually it makes the database fall over — not because there was too much work to do, but because there were too many connections competing to do it.

**How big should the fleet actually be?**

If bigger isn't better, what is the right number? The most widely cited answer comes from HikariCP, a popular Java connection-pooling library, whose maintainers derived a formula from a fairly unglamorous observation: a database server can only run a certain number of queries genuinely in parallel, bounded by how many CPU cores it has and how many separate physical disks it can read from at once. Their rule of thumb is:

connections = (CPU cores × 2) + number of disk spindles

For a typical modern database server, this works out to something startlingly small — often just 10 to 20 connections total, serving an application that might be handling thousands of requests per second. That number looks wrong at first glance to almost everyone who encounters it for the first time; it feels like it should be in the hundreds, given the request volume involved. But the formula is making a specific, defensible claim: once you've got enough connections to keep every CPU core continuously busy, additional connections aren't unlocking more parallel work, because there's no more parallel work available to unlock. They're just queuing up behind the ones already running, and every connection in that queue is adding overhead — context-switching, lock contention — without adding a corresponding increase in what actually gets done. A fleet of 15 well-used cars, running constantly, moves more travellers per day than a fleet of 200 cars stuck in gridlock on the exact roads they're clogging.

This is also why pool sizing is a genuine design decision and not just a knob to turn up when things feel slow. Get it too small and requests queue, waiting for a connection to free up. Get it too large and the database chokes on its own concurrency, potentially crashing outright. There's a real optimum in between, and it's usually much closer to "surprisingly modest" than intuition suggests.

**One fleet, many airports: PgBouncer**

The formula above assumes one application, one pool, one database. Real production systems are rarely that tidy — a typical web service might run dozens or hundreds of parallel application processes, each of which would, left to its own devices, want to maintain its own pool of connections to the same database. Multiply a sensible per-process pool size by a hundred processes and you're right back to the max_connections problem, just one layer removed.

This is where a dedicated connection pooler like PgBouncer comes in — a lightweight piece of software that sits between an application and the database, acting as a single, shared fleet manager for every process that needs a database connection, rather than letting each process manage its own private fleet.

PgBouncer's most useful trick is something called transaction-mode pooling, and it's worth understanding because it reveals something clever about how the "throttle" half of pooling actually works in practice. In the simplest kind of connection pooling, a client checks out a connection, does whatever it needs to do — potentially several queries across several minutes — and only releases the connection back to the pool once that whole unit of work is finished. That's like a car rental where each traveller books the car for the length of their whole trip, even the parts where they're just sitting at a café doing nothing with it.

Transaction-mode pooling books the car differently: a connection gets checked out only for the duration of a single database transaction — often just a handful of milliseconds — and released back to the shared fleet the instant that transaction commits, ready for the next request to use, even if it's a completely different client. Most real workloads, it turns out, spend far more time between transactions (the application thinking, formatting a response, waiting on some other service) than they spend inside one. By reclaiming the connection during all that idle time, PgBouncer lets a small, fixed fleet of database connections — the sensible 10-to-20-range number from the sizing formula — serve a much larger number of application processes, all of which believe, from their own point of view, that they have a database connection whenever they need one.

It's the same shift that made ride-hailing more efficient than the taxi model it displaced: not owning a car for the whole day, but summoning one for exactly the minutes you need it, and releasing it back to a shared pool the moment you're done. The car itself doesn't get faster or cheaper to build. It just gets used by many more people, because nobody's allowed to sit in it while it's parked.

**Why this stays invisible until it isn't**

Connection pooling belongs to a small, particular category of engineering decision: the ones that are completely optional right up until the moment they become mandatory, with very little warning in between. A small application, handling a handful of requests a minute, can get away without thinking about pooling at all — the cost of a fresh connection per query is real, but it's a rounding error against everything else happening. Nobody notices 20 extra milliseconds when the whole page loads in under a second anyway.

Then the application grows. Traffic goes from tens of requests a minute to hundreds a second, and the 20-millisecond connection tax that used to be invisible starts showing up as a meaningful chunk of total latency, multiplied across every request. Worse, without a pool actively limiting how many connections exist at once, a sudden burst of traffic can spawn far more simultaneous connections than the database was ever sized to handle — and that's the scenario where the max_connections ceiling gets hit and the database, rather than degrading gracefully, stops accepting new connections altogether. The failure doesn't ramp up gradually with load the way a slow query does. It's fine, fine, fine, and then it's an outage, because the thing that was quietly protecting the database — a sensible limit on concurrent connections — was never put in place.

That's really the whole shape of connection pooling as a piece of infrastructure: it does two jobs that look unrelated until you see them together. It's a cache, saving the expensive work of building a connection from scratch by reusing what already exists. And it's a throttle, refusing to let that reuse spiral into an unbounded pile of concurrent connections competing for the same finite database. Most explanations only mention the first job, because it's the one that's easy to picture — the rental car returning to the curb, ready for the next traveller. But the second job is the one that actually determines whether a growing application survives its own success or gets taken down by it. A fleet that's well-stocked and well-managed disappears into the background, doing its work so smoothly that nobody remembers it's there. A fleet that's mismanaged — too small or, just as dangerously, too large — becomes the whole story, the thing every postmortem traces back to. The best infrastructure, in databases as everywhere else, is the kind you only ever hear about once, on the one day it wasn't there.
