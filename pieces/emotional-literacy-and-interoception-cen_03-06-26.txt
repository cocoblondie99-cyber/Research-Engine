The Letter That Was Never Opened

Try this before you read any further. Sit still, put your hands in your lap, and without touching your wrist or your neck, count your own heartbeats for thirty seconds. Just feel for them. Count what you feel — not what you imagine the number should be. Then check against a clock and a pulse.

Some people land within a beat or two of the truth. They can feel the drum behind the ribs as plainly as they can feel a hand on the shoulder. Others count four when the real number was thirty-one, or sit there in mild bewilderment, finding nothing at all in the dark behind the sternum. This isn't a trick of fitness or attention. It's a measurement of something psychologists call interoception — the sense by which you perceive the internal state of your own body. And the spread in how people perform on this little test turns out to be a surprisingly direct window into something much larger: how well you can feel your own emotions.

That connection is the subject of this piece. Because for a great many people — and especially for those who grew up in homes where feelings were simply not part of the furniture — the heartbeat test goes badly. And the reason it goes badly is not that something inside them is broken. It's that something was never installed.

Let me explain what I mean by that, because it changes everything about how this story is usually told.

We tend to think of emotions as things that arrive. Fear shows up. Joy descends. Grief hits you. The language is all weather and intrusion, as though feelings were fully formed objects that travel from somewhere out there and land, complete, inside us. For most of the twentieth century, science broadly agreed: there were a handful of basic emotions, each with its own fixed circuit in the brain, each firing off like a smoke alarm when the right trigger appeared. Press the fear button, get fear.

The neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett spent decades looking for those buttons and couldn't find them. What she found instead was stranger and, once you sit with it, far more useful. Emotions, on her account, are not detected. They are constructed — built, in real time, by the brain, out of two raw ingredients. The first is a stream of physical signals coming up from inside the body: heart rate, the tightness or looseness of the gut, breath, temperature, the low hum of chemistry that the brain monitors constantly to keep you alive. Barrett calls this background churn "affect" — not yet an emotion, just a raw read on whether things are pleasant or unpleasant, calm or aroused. The second ingredient is meaning. The brain takes that wordless physical state and, drawing on everything it has ever learned, makes a guess about what it means right now, in this situation.

A pounding heart and a hot face, standing at the front of a quiet room, gets labelled "nerves." The very same pounding heart and hot face, standing across from someone you've wanted to kiss for months, gets labelled "desire." The same physical state. Two different emotions. The difference is the interpretation — the meaning laid over the sensation. The body supplies the energy; the mind supplies the name.

This is the part worth slowing down on, because it's the hinge the whole piece turns on. If emotions were pre-built objects, you would either receive them correctly or you wouldn't, and there'd be little to learn. But if emotions are constructed — if the brain is constantly taking a vague bodily signal and translating it into a specific feeling — then there has to be a translation layer. Somewhere between the raw flutter in the chest and the finished thought this is dread, there is a process doing the conversion. And anything that gets built can fail to get built.

So where does the translation layer come from? Nobody is born able to read their own insides. A newborn has the signals — the racing heart, the clenched gut, the flood of distress — but no idea what any of it means. The baby just feels bad, hugely and formlessly, and cries. What turns that formless badness into something legible is, in the first instance, another person.

This is the quiet machinery of childhood that almost nobody notices while it's happening. A good-enough caregiver does something extraordinary thousands of times without ever calling it anything. The child's face crumples and the parent says, gently, "Oh, you're tired, aren't you. You're all worn out." The child is frightened by a dog and the parent crouches down and says, "That was scary. That dog was very loud." The toddler is rigid with frustration over a jammed shoe and the parent names it: "It's so frustrating when it won't go on." None of this looks like teaching. It looks like ordinary soothing. But underneath the soothing, something precise is being transmitted.

Psychologists call it co-regulation, and it does two jobs at once. The first is to calm the child down — a regulated nervous system reaching across and lending its steadiness to a dysregulated one, the way a tuning fork brings another into pitch. But the second job is the one that matters for our story. Every time the caregiver names the feeling, they are handing the child a translation. This sensation — this particular churn in the body — is called frustration. That one is fear. This heavy, slow, leaking thing is sadness, and it passes, and you are still loved while it's happening. Over thousands of repetitions, the child internalises the whole dictionary. The translation layer gets built, sensation by sensation, word by word, on the scaffolding of someone else's attention.

By the time that child is grown, the process runs so fast and so far beneath awareness that it feels like perception. They don't decode their feelings; they just feel them, the way you don't decode the letters on this page one at a time but simply read. The translation has become invisible. Which is exactly why its absence is so hard to notice in the people who never got it.

Now imagine a childhood with the signals but without the naming. Not necessarily a cruel home — often the opposite, a home that looked entirely fine from the outside. The lights were on, the bills were paid, nobody was hit. But emotions were not noticed, not named, not met. A child cries and is told, not unkindly, to stop being silly. A child is anxious and the anxiety simply goes unremarked, like weather no one comments on. The parents may have been overwhelmed, or depressed, or were themselves raised in homes where feelings were furniture you weren't allowed to sit on. Whatever the cause, the effect is consistent: the child's inner states arrive, again and again, and no one reflects them back. No one says you're frightened, you're sad, you're worn out. The dictionary is never handed over.

This is the landscape the psychotherapist Jonice Webb named Childhood Emotional Neglect — CEN — and its signature in adulthood is peculiar and specific. The body still works perfectly. The heart still races, the gut still clenches, the throat still tightens, the chest still floods. The signals fire exactly as they're meant to. What's missing is the reader. These adults grow up with a body that speaks fluently and a mind that cannot understand a word it says.

There's a clinical name for the symptom, too: alexithymia, from the Greek — literally, "no words for feelings." It isn't an absence of emotion. People high in alexithymia feel as much as anyone; the physiology is all there. What they lack is the capacity to identify what they're feeling and to tell one feeling from another — and, tellingly, the capacity to distinguish an emotion from a plain physical sensation. Ask them how they feel and they'll tell you they're tired, or they have a headache, or their stomach hurts, or simply that they feel "bad" or "fine" — the body's bandwidth collapsed down to two or three coarse settings. And the research is unusually clear on the link: childhood emotional neglect strongly predicts both difficulty identifying feelings and difficulty separating those feelings from bodily sensations. The translation layer that co-regulation was supposed to build never got built, and the gap is measurable decades later.

Which brings us back to the heartbeat. It's no accident that people who struggle to name their emotions also tend to do badly on the heartbeat-counting test. The two abilities run on the same channel. If you can't feel the drumming in your chest with any accuracy, you're missing the very raw material out of which a feeling gets constructed — and if you can't feel the raw material, there's nothing to translate. The signal is sealed. It arrives, and it is never opened.

I want to stay with that image, because it is the kindest and most accurate way I know to describe what's actually going on. For the CEN adult, the problem was never that emotions don't happen. The body fires faithfully, all day, every day. The problem is that the letters pile up unopened in a language no one ever taught them to read. And this reframing matters enormously, because it moves the whole story off one track and onto another. The old story — the one these adults usually tell about themselves — is something is wrong with me. I'm cold. I'm broken. I don't feel things like other people do. The truer story is something was never taught to me. The difference between those two sentences is the difference between a defect and a gap. You cannot fix a defect. But a gap you can fill. A skill you missed, you can still learn.

So what does learning it actually look like, decades late, with no parent crouching down to name things for you?

It turns out you can build the translation layer in adulthood, but you have to come at it from the bottom up — starting not with the words, which is where most of us instinctively reach, but with the raw sensation the words are supposed to label. You have to learn to feel the heartbeat before you can learn to name what it means.

The most basic version is the body scan: lying or sitting still and moving attention slowly through the body, part by part, simply noticing what's there. Not interpreting, not fixing — just registering. Warmth here. Tightness there. A held breath. A clenched jaw you didn't know was clenched. This sounds almost insultingly simple, and for someone whose interoception is intact it nearly is. For someone whose attention has spent a lifetime trained away from the body, it can be the first time in years they've genuinely felt the inside of their own chest. There are more structured versions of the same idea — one approach developed specifically for this, Mindful Awareness in Body-oriented Therapy, or MABT, teaches people to locate, sustain attention on, and eventually find meaning in interior sensations, in that exact order, because you cannot find the meaning of a signal you cannot yet feel.

Then comes the naming — and here, remarkably, the neuroscience hands us a gift. When you take a raw, churning feeling and put a word to it — when you move from a wordless I feel awful to a specific I think this is shame, or this is grief, or this is disappointment — something measurable happens in the brain. In studies where people label their emotional states, activity in the amygdala, the structure that drives the alarm, drops; activity in the prefrontal cortex, the brain's regulating, sense-making region, rises. Researchers call it affect labelling, and the popular shorthand is "name it to tame it." The act of translation is not just record-keeping. The naming itself turns the volume down. The very thing the CEN adult was never taught to do is also one of the most reliable ways to regulate the feeling once it arrives. The skill and the relief are the same skill.

The last piece is the slowest, and it has a lovely name: emotional granularity. This is the move from a vocabulary of two or three blunt words — good, bad, fine, stressed — to a finely divided one. Learning that the thing you'd been filing under "bad" is sometimes grief and sometimes shame and sometimes plain disappointment and sometimes the particular flavour of dread that comes before something you're avoiding — and that these feel different in the body, and call for different responses. Barrett's research suggests this isn't a cosmetic improvement. People with higher emotional granularity regulate themselves better, cope more flexibly, and even show better health outcomes, because a finely labelled feeling is a feeling you can actually do something with. "I'm anxious about the meeting at three" is a problem with an edge you can get a grip on. "I feel bad" is a fog. You can prepare for the meeting. You cannot prepare for a fog.

None of this is fast, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. You are, in effect, retrofitting in your forties or sixties a system that was supposed to be built in your fours and sixes, and you're doing it without the original scaffolding of a caregiver's steady attention — which is, not coincidentally, a large part of what a good therapist quietly provides: someone to crouch down beside the feeling and help you name it, decades after the window that was supposed to do it closed. The early going can feel strange and even frightening, because feelings you've spent a lifetime not feeling don't always arrive politely once you start letting them through the door.

But here is the thing I keep coming back to, the thing that makes this one of the more hopeful stories in all of psychology. The signals were always there. That's the whole point. The CEN adult is not someone with a dead instrument trying to generate music from silence. They are someone holding a working radio, perfectly tuned, that has been playing this entire time — and slowly, patiently, learning to hear it. The letters were never lost. They were only unopened. And it is never too late to learn to read your own handwriting.
