Everyone Reported Sick at Once

It was February, and a fleet of servers had just done something that should have been reassuring and was instead catastrophic: every single instance said, in unison, that it was unwell.

The engineer who'd built the health check had done what seemed like the obvious, thorough thing. Instead of a health check that just confirmed "the process is running," they wired it to ping the database directly — a real, meaningful test. If the database answered, the service reported itself healthy. If not, unhealthy. It sounds like due diligence. It sounds like exactly what a careful engineer should do.

Then the database had one slow query. Not a crash — just a query that took longer than it should have. Every instance in the fleet, checking the same thing at the same time in the same way, hit that slowness simultaneously and concluded the same thing: I am not healthy. The orchestrator, watching those checks, did what it was designed to do when something reports itself unhealthy — it started killing and restarting instances. All of them, more or less at once. And a fleet of freshly restarted instances does what fleets of freshly restarted instances always do: it reconnects to its dependencies immediately and all together, which meant the recovering database — already limping from one slow query — got hit by a synchronized wave of reconnection traffic from every service that had just come back online. The database that caused the problem now had to survive the fix for the problem, and the fix was worse than the original slowness ever was.

The health check that was supposed to prevent an outage had manufactured one, and manufactured it more efficiently than almost anything else in the system could have.

This is a strange kind of failure, because nothing in it was obviously wrong. Nobody wrote buggy code. Nobody ignored an alert. Nobody made a lazy shortcut. An engineer built a genuinely more rigorous check than the naive version, and the rigor is what broke everything. To understand why, you have to sit with a question that looks simple and isn't: what does it actually mean for a piece of software to be "healthy"?

**The two questions hiding inside one word**

"Health check" sounds like it's answering one question. It's actually answering two, and they get confused constantly, which is exactly what happened in the outage above.

The first question is: are you alive? Is the process still running, responding, not frozen in some internal deadlock, not stuck in an infinite loop, not silently hung waiting on something that will never arrive? This is a question about the process itself. It doesn't care what the process's dependencies are doing. It only cares whether the thing in front of you is still a functioning entity at all.

The second question is completely different: are you ready? Not "are you alive" but "should I actually send you work right now, given everything currently true about your situation" — including things that have nothing to do with whether your process is running. Maybe you're alive and fine, but the database you need is down. Maybe you're alive and fine, but you're still loading a large cache into memory and would give wrong answers if asked a question this second. Maybe you're alive and fine, but you're already handling as much traffic as you can usefully hold.

These are not the same question, and the honest answer to one can be the exact opposite of the honest answer to the other. A service can be completely alive — no deadlock, no crash, no hang — while being completely unready to do its job, because the thing standing in its way is external to it. That gap between "I exist and function" and "I can currently deliver the goods" is where the February outage lived. The check was built to answer "am I ready" — it tested a real dependency — but it was wired into the machinery that answers "am I alive," which is to say, the machinery with the power to kill and restart. Asking a liveness question with a readiness check, and getting a readiness answer routed into a liveness decision, is how a single slow query became a fleet-wide restart storm.

**The triage tag**

There's a useful comparison here, and it comes from a place that has been distinguishing exactly these two questions for a very long time under much higher stakes: the emergency room.

When a patient arrives, the first thing anyone checks is crude and fast: is there a pulse? Is this person breathing? It's not a diagnosis. It doesn't ask what's wrong, how serious it is, or what treatment is needed — it asks one binary thing, and it asks it in seconds, because if the answer is no, everything else stops and someone acts immediately. That's a liveness check. It should be shallow by design. You do not want your pulse-check to depend on lab results, because lab results take time you don't have when the actual question is "is this person dying right now."

Later — assuming the pulse check passes — comes a completely different kind of assessment. Is this patient ready to be sent into surgery right now, given the state of the operating rooms, the surgical team, the equipment, everything downstream that has to be lined up for surgery to actually help them? A patient can have a strong pulse and still not be ready for the OR — maybe there's no surgeon free, maybe a prerequisite scan hasn't come back yet. "Not ready" here doesn't mean dying. It doesn't trigger an emergency response. It just means: don't route this case there yet, hold, try again shortly.

Imagine a hospital that collapsed these two checks into one — that decided a patient with a pulse who wasn't ready for surgery should be treated as if they were dying, or worse, that used the *absence* of an open OR as grounds to declare someone dead. That's an absurd category error, and stated in medical terms it's obviously absurd. Stated in infrastructure terms — a healthy process gets killed because a downstream dependency is temporarily unavailable — it's the single most common mistake in how teams build health checks, and it doesn't sound absurd at all. It sounds like thoroughness.

**Why the industry ended up with three checks, not one**

The pulse-versus-OR-readiness distinction is, in modern infrastructure, formalized as liveness versus readiness — two separate probes, checked separately, with separate consequences. A liveness check that fails gets the instance restarted, the equivalent of the emergency response. A readiness check that fails just pulls the instance out of the traffic rotation — stop sending it customers — without touching the process at all. It's allowed to be down for a while. It's allowed to say "not yet." Nobody dies over a failed readiness check; the instance just sits out until it clears.

This is why liveness checks are supposed to be shallow and dependency-free — genuinely closer to a pulse check than a physical. A good liveness check answers "has this process's internal logic gone somewhere it can't come back from" — deadlocked, hung, stuck — using information the process has about itself. It should not reach out to a database, a cache, a downstream API, or anything external, because the moment it does, the process's survival becomes hostage to something it doesn't control. That's the exact mistake from the outage story: a liveness check that depended on the database meant the database's bad day became every instance's death sentence.

Readiness checks, in contrast, are allowed — expected, even — to be deep. Checking whether the database is reachable, whether the cache is warm, whether a required downstream service is responding: that's precisely the kind of thing a readiness check exists to catch, because getting it wrong just means "don't route traffic here yet," a far gentler consequence than "kill and restart."

Kubernetes, which popularized this split, eventually added a third kind of check for a specific edge case that liveness and readiness alone didn't cover well: the startup probe. Some applications are legitimately slow to boot — loading large models into memory, warming extensive caches, running migrations — and a liveness check tuned to catch a genuinely hung process was, for these applications, also catching an application that was simply still starting. The liveness check would time out before the app had finished its one-time startup work, kill it, and the newly restarted instance would begin the same slow boot again, sometimes getting killed again before it finished — an app that could never successfully start because the safety mechanism meant to protect it kept mistaking "still loading" for "stuck." The startup probe exists to give applications that grace period explicitly: while it's active, liveness checks are held off, and only once startup succeeds does the ordinary liveness check take over. It's a purpose-built answer to "still starting" being neither "alive and fine" nor "dead," a category the original two-check model didn't quite have room for.

**The two ways to get it wrong**

Once you see health checks as small theories of what "okay" means, the two classic failure modes sitting at either end of the spectrum become easy to name.

At one extreme is the check that always says yes. An endpoint that returns "200 OK" the instant it receives a request, checking nothing at all beyond "did my web server framework manage to respond." This kind of check is worse than no check, in a sense, because it actively produces false confidence — it will happily report a service as perfectly healthy while that service is unable to reach its database, its cache is empty, and every real request it receives is failing. The orchestrator sees green across the board and does nothing, while users see errors. The check exists, technically, and tells you nothing true.

At the other extreme is the check that verifies everything, every time. An endpoint that, on every single health check request, reaches out and confirms that the database is up, the cache is up, every downstream service is up — and if any service's health check calls the health check of another service, which calls another, you've built a chain where a single slow link anywhere in it can time out the entire diagnostic exercise. This is the shape of the February failure generalized: depth in your health check isn't free, and depth wired into the wrong kind of check (liveness rather than readiness) turns your safety mechanism into a single point of coordinated failure, because now every instance is asking the same fragile question at the same time and will get the same answer at the same time.

The honest middle is neither extreme: liveness stays shallow and self-contained, checking only what the process can verify about itself, so a downstream problem never gets misread as "this process must die." Readiness is allowed real depth, because a readiness failure has a proportionate, gentle consequence — sit this one out, try again shortly, no restart required. And where a startup phase genuinely needs more time than either of those checks assumes, that gets its own explicit accommodation rather than quietly abusing liveness tolerances to cover for it.

**What "honest" actually means here**

Go back to the question underneath all of this: what does it mean for a health check to be honest? It's tempting to think honesty means depth — check more things, verify more thoroughly, don't just rubber-stamp "I'm running" and call it a day. The February outage shows why that instinct, applied without the liveness/readiness distinction, backfires so completely: the check in that story wasn't dishonest. It was, if anything, more honest than a shallow ping — it was telling the truth about the database being slow. The dishonesty was structural, not factual: it answered a true thing to the wrong question, wired into a system that would treat "my database is having a bad moment" as equivalent to "I personally am dying and must be replaced."

A genuinely honest health check doesn't just report accurate facts — it reports the *right* fact for the *decision being made with it*. "Am I alive" needs an honest answer to a narrow, self-contained question, checked shallowly, because the consequence of a "no" is drastic and immediate. "Am I ready" needs an honest answer to a much broader question, checked as deeply as the situation warrants, because the consequence of a "no" is mild and recoverable. Collapsing them doesn't make the system more honest. It makes the system unable to say the one sentence that actually mattered in February: I'm fine, but my database isn't — so don't kill me, just don't send me anyone for a minute.

That sentence — the ability to distinguish a problem in you from a problem in the world around you, and to respond to each proportionately — turns out to be a small, unglamorous piece of design with an outsized effect on whether a bad five minutes stays a bad five minutes, or becomes the whole system's worst night.
