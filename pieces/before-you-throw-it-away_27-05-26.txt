Before You Throw It Away

There is a specific kind of drawer most households have — the garment morgue. It contains the jumper with a moth hole the size of a thumbnail, the jeans with a seam that split somewhere around last October, the trousers that gave out at the inner thigh with a suddenness that felt personal. Nothing wrong with any of them except the one thing that keeps you from wearing them. They sit there because you feel vaguely guilty about throwing them away and genuinely uncertain how to fix them.

This piece is the permission and the method. Mending is not a lost art that requires specialist skills. It is two techniques, some thread, and about forty minutes of patience.


Why things fail where they do

Before you can fix a garment, it helps to understand why it failed. Most garment damage happens in the same places for the same reasons: friction, tension, and fabric meeting repeated stress at a point where it was already thin.

Trouser inner thighs are the canonical example. The fabric there is caught between two demands — it must stretch with every stride, and it takes friction from both thighs rubbing together with every stride. Quality trousers are often cut with a gusset, a small diamond of extra fabric at the crotch point, specifically to distribute that stress. Cheaper trousers skip the gusset, the seam is cut close to the body, and the fabric wears through faster. The failure point is usually the seam itself — the threads give out before the fabric does — or, in trousers worn until the fabric thins, the material between the seams rather than the seams themselves.

Elbows, collar edges, cuffs, underarms: same story. Repeated friction and movement stress the same small areas over years until something yields. A garment that has worn through in these places has not failed — it has done its job for a long time. The correct response is not a bin bag.


The two techniques

Every mending project requires one of two approaches, and picking the wrong one makes the repair worse.

Darning rebuilds fabric from within. It is the technique for knits — sweaters, socks, jersey T-shirts — where the cloth is made of interlocking loops rather than woven threads. When a hole appears in a knit garment, the surrounding fabric is structurally intact, and what you need is to recreate the missing loops or create a web of threads that covers and reinforces the area. Traditional darning uses a darning mushroom (a wooden dome that holds the fabric taut and gives you a surface to work against) and a tapestry needle, weaving threads across the hole in one direction and then stitching through them at right angles to build a flexible, durable patch of new thread embedded in the old fabric.

Patching reinforces or covers from without. It is the technique for wovens — denim jeans, linen shirts, wool trousers — where the fabric is made of interlaced warp and weft threads. A patch adds another layer of fabric behind or over the damaged area, held with stitches around the perimeter. Iron-on interfacing can add stability to the underlying fabric; sewing through it by hand or machine locks the repair permanently.

The key distinction is this: darn knits, patch wovens. A patch on a sweater creates a rigid island in flexible fabric and eventually tears at the edges where they meet. A darn on denim leaves a loose web of threads that does not hold against the lateral stresses woven fabric faces. Matching the technique to the cloth is ninety percent of the skill.


Repairing a seam

A seam that has simply come apart — where the thread broke but the fabric is intact — is the easiest repair in all of mending. Place the fabric right sides together, aligning the original seamline, and sew it closed with a backstitch: push the needle through from back to front, then insert it a stitch-length back and bring it up a stitch-length ahead. This creates an interlocking chain of thread that is stronger than a running stitch because each stitch is reinforced by the one that follows it.

Secure the start and end with two or three stitches worked over each other. Trim any loose thread. Turn the garment right-side out, press the seam flat with an iron, and you are done. The repair is invisible, takes ten minutes, and extends the garment's life by years.

For a seam that has torn because the fabric around it has weakened — where the material itself has frayed or thinned at the stitch line rather than the thread simply breaking — the repair requires reinforcement before you re-sew. Iron a strip of fusible interfacing to the wrong side of the weakened area, let it cool and bond, then sew the new seam slightly inside the old one, catching the interfacing in the stitching. This gives the new seam something solid to hold.


The worn-through patch

For fabric that has worn through entirely — the denim that thinned to near-transparency before giving up, the trouser thighs that are simply gone — you need a patch. Cut a piece of similar-weight fabric slightly larger than the hole on each side by at least a centimetre. If you want the repair invisible, use fabric from a seam allowance or a hem of the same garment. If you do not care about invisibility — and increasingly, people do not — use contrasting fabric and make the patch a feature.

Place the patch behind the hole on the wrong side of the fabric. If you are machine-stitching, run parallel rows of straight stitch across the patch and the damaged area, spacing them about two millimetres apart, working in one direction and then rotating ninety degrees to work across in the other. This is essentially darning with a sewing machine — you are recreating a grid of thread that spans the hole and binds the patch in place. The result is dense, flexible, and nearly indestructible. By hand, the same grid of stitches works at a slightly larger scale, just more slowly.

For the inner thigh specifically, the patch goes on the wrong side before you re-stitch the seam, which means you are reinforcing the area and closing the gap in one operation. If the hole has spread wide, you may need to trim the edges to a manageable shape before patching — a rough ragged tear is harder to work with than a clean-edged area of missing fabric.


Reinforcing before the hole appears

The best mending happens before the garment needs it. If you examine your trousers and find the inner thigh fabric thinning — lighter in colour than the surrounding cloth, slightly translucent when held up to a window — you have a window of perhaps a season to patch before you have an emergency. Iron interfacing to the wrong side of the thinned area and stitch a grid of running stitches across it. The fabric is reinforced, the future hole is prevented, and no one can tell.

This is counterintuitive — most people patch only after the damage, not before it. But treating a garment as something worth maintaining, examining it seasonally, reinforcing stress points before they fail, changes your relationship to it in a way that is quietly satisfying. You are attending to something rather than simply using it until it breaks.


The visible mending movement

There is a growing school of thought that visible mending is not compromise but craft. The Japanese tradition of sashiko — geometric running stitches worked through multiple layers of cloth, originally used to reinforce working garments — produces textiles of real beauty from what began as a purely practical technique. The associated philosophy of boro, making do with layered textile scraps and repairs accumulated over generations, has been exhibited in museums. The mending is not hidden. It is the point.

The western visible mending revival is less formally codified but points in the same direction: a darn in bright contrast wool against a grey sweater, a patch of floral cotton behind ripped denim, a seam re-sewn in a colour that announces itself. These repairs do not pretend the garment was always perfect. They announce that the owner valued it enough to bring it back. There is something honest about a garment that carries its history visibly. It has been somewhere. It has been worn. It has been kept.


The drawer

The drawer full of almost-garments has a straightforward fix, and it costs almost nothing — a needle, some thread, thirty minutes at most. The jumper comes back. The jeans get worn again. The trousers, once you have reinforced the seam and patched the lining, may well outlast a replacement by several years.

There is a particular pleasure in wearing something you have fixed. The garment becomes specifically yours — repaired by your hands, kept alive by your attention. A new identical garment from the same shop does not carry that. The relationship between a mended thing and its owner is different in kind from the relationship between a consumer and their wardrobe. One is a collection of objects. The other is a history of decisions to keep going.
