Unfinished Business

A person can describe their trauma in precise, clinical detail. The date. The room. The exact words spoken, and by whom, and in what tone. They can deliver the whole account without stumbling — articulate, accurate, composed. And then, at three in the morning, their heart hammers them awake for no apparent reason. A stranger raises a hand in a doorway and their body is already flinching before the thought catches up. The story is perfectly intact. The body hasn't heard a word of it.

This is not a failure of insight. It is not a failure of willpower or language or intelligence. It is something stranger and more specific: a split between the part of you that narrates experience and the part of you that survives it. And understanding that split — really understanding it, not just nodding at it — turns out to be the key to grasping why trauma healing so often stalls when it approaches through the front door of language, and what it looks like to approach from the bottom up.


The Brain That Goes Offline in an Emergency

The brain is not a single instrument. It is more like three instruments that evolved at different times, stacked inside one another, and pressed into collective service. At the core is the brainstem — the oldest part, shared with reptiles, responsible for the basics: breathing, heart rate, the reflexive responses that keep you alive. Wrapped around that is the limbic system, which handles emotion and threat assessment, including the amygdala — the structure that sounds the alarm. Above that sits the neocortex, including the prefrontal cortex, where language, planning, reasoning, and narrative live.

Under ordinary circumstances all three work in concert. But trauma is not an ordinary circumstance. When the amygdala detects a genuine threat — the kind that registers as survival-level danger — it does something useful and alarming: it partially takes the prefrontal cortex offline. Language and narrative require slow, sequential, deliberate processing. Survival doesn't have time for that. So the thinking, narrating, meaning-making brain is effectively stood down, and the primal systems take over.

This is why traumatic memory has such a different texture from ordinary memory. Ordinary memories are stored as stories with a beginning, middle, and end. Traumatic memories are stored as sensation, as posture, as fragments — because at the moment they were encoded, the part of the brain responsible for story was only partially running. The trauma lives in the body's implicit memory, in the brainstem and the nervous system and the muscles, not in the narrative-brain where words can reach it.

So when a therapist asks someone to talk through what happened, they are, in effect, asking the narrative-brain to explain itself to an audience that does not speak its language. The narrative-brain can comply — it can produce an account, even a moving and honest one — but the survival-brain is not in the room for that conversation. The alarm is still armed, and the verbal therapy proceeds in the room next door.


The Spring Under Tension

Here is the most useful way to think about what trauma does to the body's alarm systems.

Imagine that the body's survival response is a coiled spring. When a threat arrives, the spring loads — muscles brace, breath shortens, the heart accelerates, the autonomic nervous system charges up for action. All of that energy has a destination: fight, flee, or freeze. These are not choices. They are brainstem-level strategies that predate conscious thought by several hundred million years.

In a complete threat cycle, the spring releases. The threat is resolved — you run and escape, or you fight and prevail, or the predator passes and the freeze unlocks. Mammals are built to discharge the activation, and the visible sign of this is something most people find surprising: they shake. After escaping a predator, a gazelle will tremble violently for a minute or two before walking calmly away to graze. The trembling is not distress. It is the spring uncoiling. It is the nervous system releasing the stored charge so that it can return to baseline.

Trauma, in the body's terms, is a spring that never got to fully uncoil. The threat cycle didn't complete. The energy that was loaded for survival got interrupted — because the situation was too overwhelming, because there was no safe exit, because the threat was ongoing, or because social and cultural pressures required the person to hold themselves together. The charge remained, and the body found a way to contain it: through muscular bracing, through autonomic dysregulation, through a low-level mobilisation that never switches off. The nervous system is still, at some level, in the threat. It just learned to carry it quietly.

Insight can describe the spring perfectly. It can name the loading event, trace the moment the completion was interrupted, identify the patterns the compressed spring produces in adult life. What it cannot do, on its own, is release the tension. The spring doesn't care about the description. It is waiting for a physical event — not a conclusion, but a completion.


What "The Body Keeps the Score" Actually Means

Bessel van der Kolk's phrase became so ubiquitous that it risks becoming wallpaper — a thing people say rather than a thing people understand. What does the body actually keep, and how?

The keeping happens across several systems. Muscles develop what Wilhelm Reich called armoring — chronic holding patterns that function as a kind of psychological protection encoded in tissue. The psoas muscle, which runs from the lumbar spine through the pelvis, is often called the "fear muscle" because it contracts in the fetal protective curl and can hold that contraction for years. The jaw, the shoulders, the throat — all common sites for chronic bracing. These are not symbolic metaphors. They are measurable holding patterns in the musculature.

The autonomic nervous system keeps score by recalibrating its baseline. After prolonged threat, what feels like neutral is actually a state of low-level sympathetic activation — a readiness for danger that the person stops noticing because it has become normal. The nervous system's thermostat has been adjusted. The set-point for calm is now higher than it should be.

The vagus nerve — the long wandering nerve that connects the brain to the heart, lungs, gut, and face — keeps score through changes in what Stephen Porges calls vagal tone. High vagal tone means the nervous system can move flexibly between activation and rest. Trauma reduces vagal tone, making the nervous system stiffer, more reactive, slower to recover. Heart rate variability, which is a measurable proxy for vagal tone, is demonstrably lower in people with trauma histories.

And then there is the broader autonomic pattern that Porges described in polyvagal theory: the nervous system running protective strategies (sympathetic mobilisation for fight-or-flight, dorsal vagal shutdown for freeze and dissociation) as defaults rather than as emergency responses. The body has learned to treat ordinary experience as a potential emergency. This is adaptive intelligence — a survival system doing exactly what it was designed to do. It is also exhausting, and it makes genuine rest, connection, and presence feel structurally unavailable.

The popular version of "the body keeps the score" sometimes implies that trauma is stored in muscle and fascia in a quasi-mystical way that can be released through touch or movement alone. This oversells it. The tissue patterns are real, but the mechanism is nervous-system regulation — you are working with the body because the body is where the autonomic nervous system expresses itself and where its re-education has to happen. The spring is a useful model precisely because it locates the problem in the nervous system's incomplete discharge, not in the tissue itself.


The Principle Under Every Modality

There are now several well-developed approaches to trauma work that operate from the bottom up — that is, that address the brainstem and the autonomic nervous system before, or alongside, the narrative. They are called by different names and have their own theoretical languages, but they share a single core principle. It is worth stating it plainly: trauma is a survival response that never got to finish. Healing, at the primal level, means giving the body conditions in which it can safely complete what was interrupted.

Every bottom-up modality is an application of that one idea.

Somatic Experiencing, developed by Peter Levine, works with the spring metaphor most explicitly. Levine's insight — derived partly from studying how animals recover from threat — was that traumatic activation can be discharged through small, tracked movements if the nervous system is guided to them at a pace it can tolerate. The person is not asked to re-enter the traumatic memory; they are asked to notice what is happening in the body right now, in the present, in small doses. The emphasis is on titration: never approaching the full charge directly, but working around the edge of it until the system can release in manageable increments.

This connects to the concept Levine named pendulation — the therapeutic rhythm of moving between a "trauma vortex" (the activated, charged material) and a "healing vortex" (a resource: a felt sense of safety, a calm body part, a pleasant memory). The nervous system expands its window of tolerance not by being immersed in the difficult material but by oscillating between the difficult and the bearable, over and over, in small tolerable arcs. Each oscillation teaches the nervous system something: that activation is survivable, that there is a way back to regulation, that the threat eventually ends. This is information the survival-brain can receive. It does not receive it through being told. It receives it through experience.

TRE — Tension and Trauma Releasing Exercises — approaches the same goal through a different mechanism. It deliberately induces neurogenic tremoring: the same involuntary shaking seen in that post-escape gazelle. The tremoring is activated through a series of exercises that fatigue the leg muscles, producing spontaneous vibration that begins at the legs and travels through the body. The shaking is not distressing once you understand what it is — it is the nervous system doing exactly what it evolved to do when a threat has passed. The disruption is that modern humans have learned to suppress the tremoring reflex (it looks undignified; it feels out of control), which means the discharge mechanism that other mammals use automatically is chronically blocked. TRE deliberately unblocks it.

Both approaches share the same underlying intelligence: the body has discharge mechanisms built in. The goal is not to install something new. The goal is to create conditions safe enough for the mechanisms that were interrupted to complete.


The Sequence Matters

One reason bottom-up work can feel confusing from the outside — and why simply reading about somatic approaches rarely produces transformation — is that there is an implicit sequence to the healing, and it runs from primal to complex.

You cannot do sophisticated relational repair if the nervous system is still running in survival-mode. You cannot build earned secure attachment while the brainstem is treating neutral social cues as potential threats. You cannot metabolise shame productively if the window of tolerance is so narrow that any activation sends the system into shutdown. The primal layers have to settle enough to make the higher-order work possible, not as a prerequisite but as a ground.

This is what "bottom-up" actually means as a therapeutic approach. It is not that the cognitive and narrative work is unimportant — it is that there is an order of operations. Build enough regulation in the brainstem and autonomic nervous system that the person can actually stay present with difficult material. Widen the window of tolerance through titrated, oscillating work. Then the narrative work, the relational work, the meaning-making — all of that becomes more possible, more durable, because the primal alarm is not drowning it out.

In practice, this often means starting not with the trauma itself but with resources. A felt sense of safety in a calm moment. The experience of being regulated in the presence of a regulated other — because the nervous system is fundamentally social, built to co-regulate, and cannot be expected to find its way back to safety in isolation. Learning to notice the body's signals before they become overwhelming: the slight tightening in the chest that is the first syllable of a threat response, not the full sentence. Titration begins long before the activated material is approached.


What the Completion Feels Like

People who have experienced genuine somatic discharge often describe it in terms that sound strange to those who have not: a wave of heat, a spontaneous crying that doesn't feel like sadness, a shaking in the limbs that resolves into a peculiar calm, a sensation of something releasing in the chest or throat. These are not performances or metaphors. They are, as best as current understanding can map them, the physiological signature of an interrupted survival response reaching completion.

What follows is different from the relief of having talked something through. It has a different texture — quieter, more embodied, less like having reached a conclusion and more like having put something down. People often report that the memory in question doesn't change in content but changes in charge. They can recall it without the nervous system mobilising in response. This is sometimes called memory without the body — the story remains accessible but the primal alarm is no longer attached to it.

This is what distinguishes completion from insight. Insight says: I understand what happened, why it happened, how it shaped me. Completion says: the charge that was loaded in that moment has found its way out. The spring has uncoiled. Both matter. They work at different layers. A person can have enormous insight with little completion, and live with the physical toll of that uncoiled tension for decades. A person can have significant completion without ever fully narrating the experience — trauma in very early childhood, before language, is often in this category.

The point is not to choose between understanding and the body. The point is to know that there are two kinds of work to do, operating on different systems, and that the primal one tends to need to go first.


The Body Was Always the Right Address

There is something quietly radical in the bottom-up model. It places the site of healing not in understanding — not in the elegant explanation or the hard-won narrative — but in the tissue, the autonomic nervous system, the brainstem. In the parts of a person that were there before language, that know nothing of insight, that have been waiting patiently for an event that never arrived.

The spring model is useful here not because it reduces trauma to something mechanical but because it corrects a false expectation: that describing the spring accurately should be enough to release it. Understanding is not the same as safety, and safety — felt in the body, not reasoned — is what allows the nervous system to let go of what it has been holding. The path there is not a lecture. It is practice, titrated and repeated, in conditions that are gentle enough that the body can do what bodies know how to do.

The body hasn't heard the story. But it has been waiting, for years, for the right conditions to finish a sentence that was interrupted before it could complete.

Give it the conditions, and it knows how to find the ending.
