Two Real Fires a Month

In 1949, an inventor named Al Gross patented a device he thought doctors would love: a small radio receiver that could reach them anywhere, at any hour, so a hospital could summon a physician the moment a patient took a turn for the worse. He called it the pager. Doctors, when he pitched it to them, were not enthusiastic. Some hospitals actively discouraged their staff from carrying one. The problem wasn't the technology — it worked fine — it was what it implied about a doctor's life. Being reachable at 3 a.m., forever, on the off chance that something went wrong, sounded less like a convenience and more like a sentence with no release date. It took until the 1970s, and a lot of institutional pressure, before pagers became standard medical equipment.

Seventy-five years later, a different kind of professional carries a different kind of pager, and has exactly the same reaction to it. Software engineers on "on-call" rotation get paged when something breaks in production — a server falls over, a database fills up, a checkout flow starts failing — at any hour, including the ones they were supposed to be asleep for. Ask an engineer how they feel about being on-call and you will often get something close to what those 1950s doctors said: not fear of the work itself, but dread of the open-ended availability, the sense that your evenings and weekends now belong partly to a phone that might go off.

The instinctive fix is to treat this as a personality problem or a scheduling problem. Some people are calmer under pressure; rotate them more. Pay a stipend for the inconvenience. Hire more people so everyone's "on" less often. These are not bad ideas, and most well-run engineering organizations do some version of all three. But they treat the wrong layer of the system. On-call, as it turns out, is not primarily a human-resilience problem. It's the visible end of a chain of decisions that starts much earlier and much further away from the person holding the phone — decisions about what counts as worth waking someone up for. Follow that chain backward and something that looks like a workplace-wellness issue turns into an engineering-design issue, which is a far more solvable kind of problem.

**Start at the end of the chain: the burned-out engineer**

The most direct evidence that something is wrong with on-call as commonly practiced comes from a 2023 mixed-methods study of 35 incident responders — people whose job includes being paged when production systems fail. More than half met the clinical criteria for burnout. That's a striking number on its own, but the more useful finding is what burnout correlated with. It wasn't simply hours worked, or number of pages received. It was workload combined with low control over the work, poor teamwork, and inadequate recognition for doing it. In other words, two engineers could receive the same number of 3 a.m. pages in a month, and one would be fine while the other burned out, depending on whether they felt they had any say in how the work was structured, whether their team had their back, and whether anyone noticed the toll it took.

That's an important correction to the "just build tougher engineers" instinct, but it's not yet the whole chain. Control, teamwork, and recognition are organizational variables — they matter, but they're downstream of something even more concrete: how many of those pages were actually necessary in the first place. An engineer who gets paged twice a night for real, unavoidable emergencies is having a fundamentally different experience than one who gets paged eight times a night, six of which turn out to be nothing. The second engineer has less control (they can't predict which pages matter), worse teamwork dynamics (nobody trusts the alerts, so everyone second-guesses everyone), and no sense of recognition (there's no glory in triaging noise). Burnout isn't just a workload problem. It's frequently a signal-to-noise problem wearing a workload costume.

**One step back: the noisy alert**

This is where Google's Site Reliability Engineering practice — probably the most influential documented approach to running production systems at scale — draws a line that most teams don't. Google's SRE Workbook treats two to three *actionable* incidents per on-call shift as the sustainable ceiling. Not two to three notifications. Two to three things that actually required a human to do something. Below that, an on-call engineer can stay sharp, engaged, and rested enough to respond well when something genuine comes up. Above it, something in the system starts to give.

The average on-call engineer, across the industry, receives more than 30 alerts per shift. That's not two or three multiplied by a bit — it's an order of magnitude past the line Google considers sustainable. And the standard guidance for keeping this under control is to hold non-actionable alerts — the ones that don't require any response, that resolve themselves, that were false alarms or duplicate notifications about the same underlying issue — under 20% of total volume. Do the arithmetic on 30+ alerts a shift with an 80% actionable rate and you get a workload most teams would consider absurd if it were described to them directly, rather than arriving one ping at a time.

Here's the part that matters most: a noisy alerting system doesn't make engineers more careful. It makes them numb. This is one of the best-documented findings in the study of human attention, and it has an old, unglamorous illustration sitting in most kitchens. A smoke detector that goes off every time someone makes toast doesn't get fixed. It gets muted, or its battery gets pulled, right up until the night it detects a real fire and nobody moves — because the sound stopped meaning "emergency" months ago and started meaning "somebody's making toast again." Alerting systems degrade the same way, just more quietly. An engineer who has been paged four times this week for a disk-usage warning that always resolves itself on its own starts treating the fifth one the same way — glance, dismiss, back to sleep — and there is no reliable moment where that habit announces itself as dangerous before it actually is. The numbness isn't a character flaw. It's the predictable, almost mechanical output of a detector that was never tuned to only cry wolf when there's a wolf.

**Another step back: why the alert exists at all**

So if the alert is the problem, why not just... alert less? This is where most naive fixes to on-call fail, because turning down the sensitivity of a noisy system is exactly as risky as it sounds — you can silence the toast-smoke alarms and the real-fire alarms with the same knob if you're not careful. The actual fix has to happen earlier: at the moment someone decides what an alert should be *for*.

This is where Google's SRE model introduces a second concept that reframes the whole problem: toil. Toil, in the SRE vocabulary, is a specific category of work — manual, repetitive, reactive, automatable, and providing no lasting value once it's done. Responding to a routine on-call page that requires the same three steps every time, with no permanent fix applied, is close to the textbook definition. SRE practice caps toil at 50% of an engineer's time, as a matter of explicit policy — not a suggestion, a budget that gets tracked. If a team's on-call load is mostly toil, that's treated as a bug in the system's design, something to be engineered away, not a fact of working life to be endured with better coping strategies. A team that blows past its toil budget doesn't get told to be more resilient. It gets told to stop shipping features until it fixes its alerts.

This is a genuinely useful reframe, because it turns "on-call is exhausting" from a complaint about people into a complaint about a specification. Every alert that fires represents a decision someone made, at some earlier point, about where to draw a threshold: this metric, past this value, means something is wrong enough that a person should be interrupted, wherever they are, whatever they're doing. That decision usually gets made in isolation, by whoever was building the monitoring for a particular service, often without much thought for who would be the one woken up by it, or how often, or what else might be competing for that same person's 3 a.m. attention from six other services with their own thresholds set by six other engineers who also weren't thinking about it holistically. Alert quality isn't something that degrades on its own. It's something nobody designed on purpose, assembled instead from dozens of well-meaning, uncoordinated, slightly-too-cautious individual choices, each of which seemed reasonable in isolation and none of which accounted for the cumulative weight.

**The full chain, laid end to end**

Put the whole thing together and it runs like this. A burned-out engineer is usually someone who has lost control over which pages matter — not someone who simply lacks resilience. That loss of control is usually caused by alert noise: too many pages that turn out to be nothing, drowning out the ones that don't. That noise is usually caused by alerting thresholds that were set too conservatively, or too locally, or too long ago to reflect how the system actually behaves now. And those thresholds were usually set by an engineer, weeks or months before any 3 a.m. page, who was thinking about covering their own risk on a single metric for a single service, not about the cumulative interrupt-load on whoever ends up holding the pager.

This is the reframe the whole piece has been building toward: "fix on-call" is really "fix the alerts," and "fix the alerts" is really "fix the system that generates them" — a question of engineering discipline applied *before* the incident, not stress management applied *during* it. That's a much more tractable problem than it first appears, because unlike "build engineers who don't get worn down by chronic sleep disruption and constant low-grade threat monitoring" (which is, bluntly, not really available as an intervention), "audit every alert and ask whether it required a human to act, and if not, why it's paging one" is a concrete, bounded, repeatable engineering task. Teams that do this systematically — reviewing every page after the fact, deleting or automating the ones that didn't need a person, tightening thresholds that were catching noise instead of signal — routinely cut their alert volume by half or more within a few review cycles, without missing more real incidents. The signal was there the whole time. It was just buried.

**Why this stays a hard sell anyway**

None of this is exactly secret. Every mature engineering organization has some version of "reduce alert fatigue" written into its practices somewhere. The reason it persists as a problem anyway is that fixing an alert is boring, unglamorous, unrewarded work — deleting a threshold, tuning a rule, adding a five-minute delay before a transient spike pages someone — compared to shipping the feature that made the on-call load worse in the first place. Nobody gets promoted for the alert that didn't fire. The asymmetry is exactly backward from what would produce a healthy system: the work that prevents 3 a.m. pages is invisible and unrewarded, while the work that creates new services (and therefore new alerting surface, and therefore new potential 3 a.m. pages) is the visible, celebrated kind. Left to its own devices, that asymmetry guarantees that alert volume creeps upward over time even at organizations that genuinely care about the people carrying the pager — which is exactly why SRE practice makes toil a tracked, capped, policy-enforced budget rather than a value everyone's supposed to hold in their heads. Good intentions don't survive contact with an incentive structure that rewards the opposite behavior; only a budget does.

**What this changes**

The value of tracing the chain all the way back is that it moves the moral weight of on-call off the person answering the page and onto the person — or, more accurately, the accumulated, uncoordinated decisions of dozens of people — who decided what would make it ring. An engineer who seems to be struggling with on-call is very often not struggling with stress tolerance at all. They're the last stop on a supply chain of decisions nobody reviewed, absorbing the cost of thresholds set months ago by people who never met them and never expected to be the reason someone's phone buzzed at 3 a.m.

That's a genuinely different way to see the smoke detector on your own kitchen wall, too, once you notice the pattern: the alarm going off isn't really about the toast. It's about whoever mounted it too close to the stove and never came back to move it. The alarm is just doing exactly what it was built to do. The building of it is where the real decision happened — and where the real fix has to happen too.
