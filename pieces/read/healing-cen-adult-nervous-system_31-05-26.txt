What Was Never There

The standard model of recovery from emotional damage runs roughly like this: something bad happened, it left a mark, and healing means working backwards to undo or soften the mark until something like the original self re-emerges. This model makes sense for a lot of suffering. But childhood emotional neglect doesn't fit it — because with neglect, there is no before. The wound isn't something that happened. It's the accumulated weight of everything that didn't.

When a child grows up without consistent emotional attunement — without a caregiver who reliably noticed, named, and responded to their inner life — the nervous system doesn't develop the full range of capacities it would have built in a more responsive environment. The scaffolding never went up. Which means the adult who eventually arrives in a therapist's office, or who reads a book about childhood neglect and recognises themselves in every page, isn't trying to get back to a previous state. They're trying to build, for the first time, something that was never constructed.

This distinction matters enormously for how recovery works — and it makes the road considerably stranger than the standard account suggests.


The mechanism of healing from most psychological wounds involves what researchers call corrective emotional experience. The term was coined in the 1940s by psychoanalyst Franz Alexander, and the idea is deceptively simple: the nervous system learns by prediction. It develops internal models of how emotional situations will unfold — show vulnerability, get dismissed; need comfort, receive nothing; express feeling, face shame — and it runs those models automatically, prepopulating every new relationship with the dynamics of the old ones.

A corrective experience is the moment when the model turns out to be wrong. You show vulnerability and the other person moves toward you rather than away. You ask for help and it arrives. You make a mistake in a relationship and the relationship doesn't end. The nervous system, which was braced for the familiar negative, receives something it wasn't predicting — and updates. Not all at once. Not permanently after a single instance. But the update happens, and it accumulates.

This is why the therapeutic relationship is often described as the medicine, not merely the container for the medicine. A good therapist isn't primarily a guide explaining your past to you. They are a person with whom your nervous system gets to have different experiences, repeatedly, over time. The moment a therapist notices they've missed something and says so — "I don't think I quite caught what you were actually saying there, can we go back?" — that is not a small thing. It is a rupture followed by a repair. For a nervous system trained to expect that ruptures simply accumulate and nobody comes back, a survived and repaired misattunement is extraordinary data.


But here is where childhood emotional neglect creates its specific complication. The whole enterprise of healing through relational experience requires you to be inside relational experience — to be present in moments of connection, to feel them, to let them register. And CEN specifically damages this capacity above almost all others.

What emotional neglect leaves behind, more than fear or anger or grief, is a kind of relational numbness. The person who was never seen gradually stopped expecting to be seen, and then stopped noticing whether they were or weren't, and eventually may have lost much of their access to the felt sense of connection entirely. They arrive in relationships — including therapeutic ones — and feel vaguely nothing. Not safe. Not unsafe. Just absent from the room inside themselves.

The polyvagal framework calls this dorsal vagal shutdown — the most ancient emergency response of the nervous system, one that doesn't fight or flee but simply goes quiet. Think of a possum playing dead. The threat was too consistent and too chronic to fight, so the system learned to power down. Not as a dramatic one-time response but as a default resting state. The lights are on; the person is not particularly home.

This creates what we might call a lock-and-key problem in recovery: you need relational experience to heal, but you cannot fully access relational experience because healing hasn't happened yet. The wound doesn't just hurt; it blocks the door to the remedy.


The concept that researchers use to describe the space where this matters is the window of tolerance — a phrase coined by psychiatrist Dan Siegel to describe the zone of arousal in which the nervous system can process emotional experience without becoming overwhelmed or shutting down.

Below the window is hypoarousal: the numbness, the flatness, the dissociation, the sense of being elsewhere while your body sits in the chair. Above it is hyperarousal: the panic, the flooding, the hypervigilance, the moment when everything feels too loud and too close and you need to leave or go silent or disappear. Emotional processing — the real learning and updating — can only happen inside the window. Outside it, you're either too switched-off to register experience or too overwhelmed to integrate it.

CEN tends to narrow the window significantly. The person who grew up with emotional unavailability learned to manage their nervous system in very specific ways — usually by staying flat, staying watchful, staying slightly nowhere. Significant emotional proximity feels like danger. Significant emotional withdrawal also feels like danger, because now the familiar numbness is being disturbed. The window can end up quite thin: a narrow band between the threat of being felt and the threat of being found.

Effective therapy — and effective relational healing more broadly — works by gradually widening that window. Not by forcing entry into the blocked territory, but by accumulating evidence that the window can be stretched a little without catastrophe. A small dose of connection. A small moment of being seen. The system activates slightly, is not destroyed, settles back. This is what somatic therapists call titration: not flooding but a careful introduction, like acclimatising to cold water by wading in from the shallows rather than diving.


This is where body-based approaches often prove more effective than talk therapy alone for people shaped by CEN. The attachment wound isn't primarily a cognitive problem. It's not that you hold wrong beliefs about relationships that could be corrected by better information. The encoding happened before language, before memory as most of us usually think of it, in the body's nervous system and its automatic predictions. Talking at those patterns from the outside doesn't always reach them.

Somatic Experiencing, developed by Peter Levine, works by drawing attention to bodily sensation — not emotion, not narrative, just sensation. The tension in the jaw. The slight breath-hold when someone gets too close. The heaviness in the chest that arrives just before the numbness does. Neglect-affected adults often have significant interoception deficits: they've lost touch with what their body is actually feeling, in the same way that someone in chronic mild dehydration eventually stops registering thirst. The felt sense has been suppressed for so long it goes quiet.

What somatic work does, very slowly and without forcing, is reintroduce the nervous system to itself. Not to fix it but to make contact with it. The capacity to feel connection starts with the capacity to feel anything — and for someone whose early environment was emotionally barren, this is often genuinely novel territory. The first goal isn't safety in relationships; it's noticing that your body has sensations at all.

Internal Family Systems therapy, developed by Richard Schwartz, offers a complementary approach with particular elegance for CEN recovery. The IFS framework understands the psyche as a community of parts that developed to serve specific functions. The parts that form in response to emotional neglect are mostly protective: the part that maintains emotional distance, the part that becomes hypercompetent and self-sufficient, the part that monitors constantly for signs of being too much or too little, the part that pre-empts rejection by never fully arriving in the first place. These aren't pathological. They were functional adaptations that kept the child's system manageable in an unresponsive environment.

The IFS insight is that the protectors cannot simply be dismantled by force. They exist for reasons. Pushing past them triggers more protection — which is why people with CEN can spend years in therapy that goes through the motions but never quite reaches anything real. The protectors are too good at their jobs. But they can be befriended — approached with curiosity rather than pressure, asked what they're worried about, asked what they need before they'll stand aside. Over time, as they receive acknowledgement rather than warfare, they relax their grip on the younger parts they're guarding: the exiles carrying the loneliness, the hunger for attunement that was never answered, the grief for what was consistently missing.

This is not just a metaphor. The lived experience of this work, for people doing it, is of having internal negotiations — of noticing the voice that says stop, too much, who do you think you are, and instead of obeying it or fighting it, just sitting with it and asking what it's afraid of. The protective part almost always has a childhood answer. And the adult self can offer it something the child never received: acknowledgement that it did its job, and that things are different now.


Here is the evidence that this actually changes something durable.

In the Adult Attachment Interview, a structured assessment developed by Mary Main at Berkeley in the 1980s, researchers ask adults to describe their childhood relationships — and then evaluate not the content of what happened but the coherence and integration of how they tell the story. Secure attachment in adults looks like a narrative that is balanced and specific: neither idealising the past nor consumed by bitterness about it, reflective and emotionally grounded, able to hold complexity without being overwhelmed by it.

A significant minority of adults — roughly 20 to 25 percent of those who classify as securely attached — turn out to have had childhoods that should have produced insecurity. The events were there: the unavailable parents, the emotional absence, the consistent failure of attunement. But something intervened, and their narrative coherence looks essentially indistinguishable from people who had genuinely secure childhoods from the start.

Mary Main called this pattern earned secure attachment — earned, because it wasn't given in childhood and had to be built through experience and reflection after the fact. And subsequent neuroimaging work produced a striking finding: the brains of earned-secure adults function differently from insecure adults and very similarly to people who were continuously secure throughout their lives. The prefrontal cortex is engaged in emotional regulation. The amygdala responds proportionally rather than defensively. The systems of social cognition run without the constant bracing that characterises insecure attachment.

The mechanism by which people earn security appears to involve some combination of: a reparative close relationship — a partner, a long friend, sometimes a therapist; significant reflective effort, meaning you didn't just live through the experiences but made coherent sense of them; and enough time and enough safety for the nervous system to accumulate counter-evidence. The common thread in earned-secure narratives is that people have come to understand their parents as people — limited, with their own histories and their own wounds — rather than experiencing them as the universe's authoritative judgement on their own worth.

None of this is quick. None of it is linear. The research is clear that earned-secure adults, while neurologically similar to continuously-secure adults in many respects, often carry more vulnerability to depression and anxiety than people who never needed to earn anything. The scaffold was built late, in conditions less than ideal. It holds, but some weather still gets in.


This brings us back to the question of the goal. What are you actually moving toward when you heal from childhood emotional neglect?

For PTSD — trauma as a discrete event — there is a comprehensible recovery model: the nervous system is stuck replaying a moment as if it were still happening, and the work is to help it file the memory as past, complete it, reduce its grip on the present. You are restoring something that was disrupted.

For CEN, there is nothing to restore. You cannot return to an earlier capacity you didn't have. What you are doing is developmental work in an unusual developmental environment: building, in your forties or twenties or wherever you are, emotional capacities that are typically constructed in childhood through thousands of small attunements you never received. The window of tolerance that was never widened is being widened now, through experiences you are having rather than experiences you missed. The internal working model that said I am fundamentally unknowable, or irrelevant to others, or too much, or simply not worth noticing — that model is being slowly revised not through argument but through accumulated contrary evidence.

This framing comes through clearly in the work of psychologist Diana Fosha, who developed Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy, or AEDP. Fosha's central premise is that transformation — not symptom reduction but genuine emergence of something new — is the primary process in healing. Her framework pays particular attention to what she calls transformational affects: the emotions that arise when change is actually happening, which are often surprising in their quality. Not relief, exactly. Not happiness. More like a particular startled aliveness, a sense of finding your footing somewhere you didn't expect ground to be. Fosha argues these states should be explicitly named and savoured in therapy, because they are the felt signature of the nervous system doing something it has not done before.

It is, genuinely, building something from scratch. Which is in some ways harder than repair — there is no blueprint, no memory of what it felt like to have this — and in some ways easier, because you are not fighting nostalgia for a former self or mourning the before. There is no before. There is only what is being built.


In practice, this work looks unglamorous. It looks like going to therapy and talking about what feels like nothing for several months and then one day noticing, almost as an afterthought, that something is slightly less nothing than it was. It looks like a friendship in which you manage to say one honest thing about how you are actually doing, and the other person responds without judgement, and you notice — with something between surprise and faint suspicion — what your body does when that happens: a slight unclenching, a small drop in the held quality of the breath.

It looks like beginning to register, very fleetingly, that you were actually present in a conversation instead of watching yourself have it from a slight internal distance. It looks like noticing that the voice saying stop, too close, too much is still there but is somehow no longer running the whole show.

The changes are molecular at first. They arrive without announcement. People in recovery from CEN often report only noticing the shift in retrospect — that something they used to feel constantly (the blankness, the pre-emptive distance, the faint sense of being somehow behind glass) is now less constant, less automatic, slightly more optional. The nervous system still speaks the old language. But it is slowly, haltingly, acquiring a new one.

What was never built can be built. Not the same as if it had been built at the right time, by the right people, in the right conditions. Some things that would have been effortless at three remain a considered undertaking at forty. But the fundamental claim of earned secure attachment research — that narrative coherence, brain function, and relational capacity can shift across a lifetime — holds.

You are not finding your way back to somewhere you once were. You are finding your way to somewhere you have never been. That is not a consolation. It is genuinely different terrain, genuinely new, and it belongs to you.
