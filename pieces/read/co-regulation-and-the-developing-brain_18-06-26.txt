The Building That Was Never Finished

A newborn cannot regulate its own body temperature. It cannot manage its own heart rate, cortisol levels, or breathing rhythm under stress. For a few weeks after birth, it cannot even stabilise its own sleep cycles without external cues. The body of a newborn is, in the most literal sense, a work in progress — a system that has arrived in the world before it is ready to run independently, and that relies on the bodies of others to do its regulating for it.

We know this about babies and accept it without much fuss. What we understand less clearly — what the research of the past two decades has been quietly establishing — is that the nervous system is in exactly the same position, and stays there for much longer. A child is not born with the wiring to calm itself down after distress. That wiring is built. It is assembled, synapse by synapse, across thousands of ordinary, unremarkable moments — moments of being held when frightened, soothed when overwhelmed, met when reaching out. The builder is not the child. The builder is the caregiver.

This process has a name: co-regulation. And for the many adults who grew up in households where it was absent or insufficient — households marked by childhood emotional neglect — understanding what co-regulation actually is and how it works changes everything about why they struggle with feelings, and what it might take to stop.

---

The thermostat image is useful here. Imagine a house with a fully installed heating and cooling system — the boiler works, the radiators are real, the pipes are sound. But the thermostat was never calibrated. No one set the reference point, never showed the system what "room temperature" feels like, never gave it a stable baseline from which to respond. So it swings. The boiler runs too hot and the rooms overheat; then the system overcorrects; then it doesn't respond to cold at all. The problem is not the equipment. The problem is that the equipment was never taught what normal feels like.

Co-regulation in childhood is what calibrates the thermostat. When a caregiver soothes a distressed infant — when they speak in a slow, low voice, hold the baby close, regulate their own breathing and gaze — they are not just calming this particular moment. They are doing something far more consequential: they are showing the child's nervous system what regulation feels like, from the inside. And the child's nervous system, over hundreds and thousands of such moments, begins to learn to produce that state on its own.

---

How does this actually happen in the brain? The answer involves one of the more striking findings in developmental neuroscience, a phenomenon researchers call brain-to-brain synchrony.

Using a technique called hyperscanning — which records the brain activity of two people simultaneously while they interact — researchers have found that when an attuned caregiver and infant are engaged with each other, their brain oscillations literally synchronise. Not metaphorically. Not loosely correlated. The electrical patterns in their brains begin to move together in measurable, trackable ways. When the caregiver is calm and present, the infant's neural activity entrains to theirs. The baby's brain is, in a very real sense, borrowing the adult's regulation.

This neural synchrony during early interactions predicts downstream outcomes in ways you might not expect. Higher synchrony correlates with better language development, stronger joint attention, and improved emotion regulation in later childhood. The brain-to-brain link in infancy is not just an interesting anomaly; it appears to be one of the pathways through which the building gets built.

The mechanism running underneath all of this is the autonomic nervous system — specifically, the part of it that Stephen Porges, a neuroscientist at Indiana University, has spent decades mapping. Porges's polyvagal theory describes three distinct states of the autonomic nervous system, and the one that matters most here is the ventral vagal state: the biological configuration associated with safety, social engagement, and genuine relaxation.

When you feel safe — not just intellectually safe, but viscerally, bodily safe — that is the ventral vagal system engaged. It is activated not by thought but by cues: a slow, warm voice; soft, attentive eye contact; a relaxed, open facial expression. These are the precise signals a regulated caregiver sends to a child. And when the child's nervous system detects them, the ventral vagal pathway pulls the child's own system toward that same state. This is the biological mechanism of co-regulation. It is not emotional mirroring in any vague, metaphorical sense. It is a specific physiological process in which one person's regulated nervous system uses social signalling to entrain another's.

The crucial point is that this pathway — the ventral vagal system — has to be exercised to develop. A muscle that is never used weakens. A circuit that is never activated does not strengthen. The ventral vagal pathway becomes robust through repeated use, and its use in early childhood is almost entirely dependent on the caregiver. If the caregiver is absent, dysregulated, emotionally unavailable, or simply chronically stressed — if the thousand small moments of soothing do not reliably arrive — the child's ventral vagal system does not get the training it needs.

---

Which brings us to what the adult who didn't receive adequate co-regulation is actually carrying.

The architecture of the emotional brain runs roughly like this: the amygdala is the alarm system. It is fast, it is loud, and it does not reason. When it detects threat — real or perceived — it fires. The prefrontal cortex is the part of the brain that contextualises, reasons, and can say: actually, this is not a tiger, this is just your boss with a disappointing expression. But the connection between the prefrontal cortex and the limbic system — the neural pathway that allows the thinking brain to calm the alarm — is not hardwired. It has to be developed. And one of the primary mechanisms through which it develops is, again, co-regulation. When a caregiver soothes a child repeatedly, what is being built at the neural level is exactly this: the prefrontal-to-limbic pathway, the regulatory link between reason and raw emotion.

In adults with CEN backgrounds, this link is often underdeveloped. The amygdala remains hyperreactive — overactive, easily triggered, quick to treat ambiguous stimuli as threat. The hippocampus, which is responsible for contextualising threat signals and saying this is familiar, this is not new, this has a known pattern, may be underdeveloped as a result of chronic early stress. The window of tolerance — the band of activation within which a person can function, feel, and think — is narrow.

A window of tolerance is exactly what the name suggests: a zone of arousal within which the nervous system can be present to experience without either shutting down or detonating. People with wide windows can experience quite a lot of emotional intensity and remain functional. People with narrow windows flip very quickly: from manageable to overwhelmed, from present to dissociated, from stable to shutdown, often with almost no middle ground, and often with no idea what triggered the flip.

This is not a character flaw. It is not a lack of willpower or emotional maturity or resilience. It is an architectural fact about how the nervous system was built. The upper floors of the building — the ones that were supposed to be constructed through thousands of calm, attuned interactions — were never completed. The person is running on a ground floor that was laid down in infancy, without the higher storeys that were supposed to be added over the next ten years.

This is why talking about feelings is not always sufficient. Understanding a problem intellectually and being able to regulate around it are two entirely different things, because they live in two entirely different parts of the brain. A person can know, with complete clarity, that their anger is disproportionate, that their shutdown is not a rational response to what just happened, that their flooding is not about this moment — and still not be able to stop any of it. The knowledge is in one building; the response is happening in another. Co-regulation would have built the bridge between them. Without it, the bridge has to be constructed later, by other means, which is possible but harder.

---

What does insufficient co-regulation actually look like in daily life? Some of it is obvious in retrospect: the person who cannot be comforted by anyone, because comfort was never reliably offered and the nervous system never learned to receive it. The person who flatlines under stress, goes very quiet and very still, and comes back twenty minutes or two hours later not quite sure where they went. The person who is accused of being cold, or distant, or hard to reach — not because they don't feel things, but because their feelings never developed reliable pathways to expression.

Some of it is subtler. There is a particular quality of hypervigilance that comes not from trauma events but from chronic emotional unpredictability: the person who is constantly scanning for other people's moods, interpreting micro-expressions, trying to sense the atmosphere of a room before they enter it. The nervous system learned, correctly, that the emotional environment could not be taken on trust — that safety had to be assessed and reassessed constantly, because it was never reliably provided. That vigilance was adaptive then. In adulthood, it is exhausting, and it never turns off.

There is also the problem of interoception — the sense of what is happening inside your own body. Co-regulation, when it works, does not just teach the nervous system to calm down; it teaches the body that its internal signals matter, that they will be noticed and responded to. A baby who cries and is held learns, at a cellular level, that its internal state can influence the external world, that signals sent outward produce a response. When this reliable response does not arrive, the nervous system draws a different lesson: internal signals don't matter; there is no point transmitting them; learn not to feel them. The result is interoceptive deficit — difficulty noticing and reading the body's emotional signals, which feeds directly into the alexithymia (difficulty identifying and describing feelings) that is so common in CEN adults.

---

Here is where the picture gets more complicated, and also more hopeful.

The conventional model of brain development treats early childhood as a critical period: a window in which certain wiring must be laid down, after which the window closes and what is done is done. This model is accurate for some systems — vision, language, certain auditory functions. But for the autonomic nervous system, and particularly for the ventral vagal pathway, the evidence points in a different direction. The system retains significant plasticity well into adulthood. The window does not close.

This is important enough to dwell on. The nervous system can still learn what it missed. The mechanism is the same: co-regulation, offered by another regulated nervous system, detected through social signals, experienced repeatedly until the pathway begins to strengthen. What changes is not the mechanism but the context. In childhood, it happens with a caregiver, in ten thousand unscheduled moments spread across years. In adulthood, it happens — if it happens — in therapy, or in a close relationship with someone who has sufficient regulation of their own to offer.

Porges's polyvagal theory points to why the therapeutic relationship works when it does. A therapist who is genuinely present, warm, and regulated is not just providing a good conversational environment. They are doing something physiologically specific: activating the ventral vagal system in the client through safe social cues, repeatedly, across dozens or hundreds of sessions. The client's nervous system is being given repeated experiences of what safety actually feels like, at a bodily level, with another human being. Over time, if the relationship is consistent enough and the experiences safe enough, the ventral vagal pathway begins to strengthen and the window of tolerance begins to widen.

This is not a quick process. The building is being constructed one floor at a time, and adult construction is slower than the rapid synaptogenesis of early childhood. But it is happening through the same mechanism, using the same biology. The body does not particularly care that the building is arriving late.

---

What this means practically is that insight, while necessary, is not sufficient. Understanding that you have a narrow window of tolerance does not widen it. Understanding why you go cold in conflict does not warm you up. The nervous system is changed by experience, not by understanding, and the experiences that change it are relational — they involve another body, another regulated nervous system, offering its safety to yours.

This is the unsentimental version of the hopeful conclusion. Co-regulation is not something you can do alone. It requires exactly what was missing: another person, consistently present, whose nervous system can be borrowed. For adults who grew up in homes where emotional attunement was insufficient, the task is not self-improvement in any traditional sense. It is something closer to finding, as an adult, the conditions under which the building can finally be finished — conditions that are relational by definition, because the biology demands it.

The nervous system that never received the signal can still learn to receive it. The window that was never opened can still be opened. The thermostat can still be calibrated, years after the house was built. But it takes someone else's hand on the dial.
