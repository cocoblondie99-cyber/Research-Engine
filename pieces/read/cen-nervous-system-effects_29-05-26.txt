The Body That Learned to Brace

Picture a baby, eight months old, lying in a cot in a quiet room. She is crying — not from hunger or pain, but from the particular distress of aloneness, of needing a face and finding none. No one comes. The crying intensifies. Still no one comes. Then, eventually, something shifts. Not in the room, but in the baby. The crying subsides. She goes still. Her gaze goes flat. She looks, to anyone watching, as though she has calmed down. She hasn't. She has learned something — something her nervous system will carry into adulthood without ever finding words for it.

Nothing violent happened in that room. There was no shouting, no pain, no threat in the conventional sense. And yet what happened in that baby's body — in the branching network of nerves that runs from her brainstem to every organ in her torso — is the beginning of a story that will follow her for decades. It is the story that childhood emotional neglect tells: not through events, but through absences. Not through what was done, but through what never came.

Most conversations about trauma focus on the dramatic. The event. The incident. The thing that happened. But the nervous system doesn't care much about this narrative tidiness. It responds just as profoundly to chronic absence as to acute injury. And when the absence occurs early enough — when it happens during the years in which the nervous system is still being built — its effects can be deeper and stranger and harder to name than anything a conventional understanding of trauma would predict.

This is a piece about what that absence does to the body. About how the nervous system forms, why it needs another person to do so, and what happens when that other person isn't there. About a concept called polyvagal theory that turns out to explain, with startling precision, why so many adults who grew up in emotionally quiet houses walk around with bodies that feel permanently braced for something they can't identify. And about what it looks like to undo, years later, something the body learned before it had language.


The Nervous System Is Built in Relationship

Before anything else, it helps to understand that the human nervous system is not born complete. A newborn arrives with many survival systems already online — the infant can breathe, regulate temperature, suck, cry — but the branch of the nervous system responsible for sophisticated emotional regulation, for social engagement, for the feeling of genuine safety in the presence of another person, is not yet wired. It develops across childhood. And the extraordinary thing is that it develops primarily through relationship.

This isn't a metaphor. It's physiology.

The part of the vagus nerve — the long wandering nerve that connects the brainstem to the heart, lungs, stomach, and gut — that governs higher emotional regulation begins developing in the final trimester of pregnancy and continues maturing through childhood and into the teenage years. What builds it is a process called co-regulation: the experience of having your nervous system soothed and calmed by another nervous system that is itself calm.

Think of it this way. A small child cannot regulate themselves. They are flooded by sensation — hunger, overstimulation, frustration, fear — and they have no internal mechanism for turning the flood down. They need someone else to do it for them. They need a parent to appear with a calm face, a warm voice, steady hands, and familiar smell. When that parent appears in that way — attuned, present, regulated — something happens in the child's body. Heart rate slows. Cortisol drops. The alarm system quiets. And crucially, over thousands of repetitions of this experience, the child begins to internalise it. They begin to build, slowly, the neural circuitry that will eventually let them do this for themselves.

The technical term for what a caregiver provides is external regulation. And the process by which external regulation becomes internal capacity is, essentially, what emotional development is. You learn to soothe yourself because someone soothed you. You learn to return to calm because you were brought to calm. The template is relational before it is individual.

Stephen Porges, the American neuroscientist who developed polyvagal theory in the 1990s, called this the social engagement system — the idea that the nervous system didn't just evolve fight-or-flight responses, but also evolved an entire suite of capacities specifically for connection: for reading faces and voices, for signalling safety to others, for the particular kind of calm that comes from being genuinely seen by another person. And he found that this system sits at the top of a hierarchy. It's the most sophisticated and most recently evolved of the nervous system's regulatory strategies. When it's working, it's also the first to be deployed — and the fact that it requires another person to activate it, especially in early life, is not incidental. It's the whole point.

A child who receives attuned co-regulation consistently, across thousands of ordinary moments — a parent reading their face correctly, a parent responding to distress with warmth rather than withdrawal, a parent mirroring a smile back — is having their social engagement system exercised and built. Like any capacity, it grows stronger with use. And a child who doesn't receive this — whose distress consistently meets a blank wall of non-response, whose emotions are met with indifference, irritation, or simply absence — is having a different experience. Not just emotionally, but neurologically.


Neuroception: The Alarm System Below the Alarm System

To understand what happens when attunement fails, you need one more piece of the polyvagal picture: the concept Porges called neuroception.

Perception is conscious. You look at a situation, assess it, and form a view. Neuroception happens at a level below consciousness, faster than thought, without any involvement from the thinking brain. It is the nervous system's constant, automatic, pre-cognitive scanning of the environment for cues of safety or threat.

Every moment, your nervous system is asking questions you never consciously formulate. Is this person's voice warm or flat? Is their face open or closed? Does this space feel predictable or unpredictable? Am I being seen, or am I alone? And on the basis of these assessments — which happen in milliseconds, drawing on signals as subtle as the pitch of a voice and the degree to which a smile reaches someone's eyes — the nervous system shifts its state. Not because you decided to, but because the assessment happened faster than decision-making is possible.

This system is ancient. It predates language, predates cortical reasoning, predates anything we'd recognise as conscious experience. It is borrowed wholesale from our evolutionary ancestors, who needed to know very quickly whether another animal was friend, threat, or prey. In humans it has been repurposed for social life — we use these same hair-trigger circuits to read safety in human faces, in the quality of human attention, in the texture of the social environment.

What neuroception means for the child with emotionally absent caregivers is this: every time the child reaches out and finds blankness, the neuroceptive system logs a data point. Not "my parent isn't available right now," which is a thought. But something more primitive: the absence of expected warmth registers as a cue of threat. Not consciously. Not as a story. As a signal, below language, that the environment is unsafe.

Repeat this across months, then years, and the neuroceptive system doesn't just log individual instances. It calibrates. It adjusts its baseline. It starts, at a pre-conscious level, expecting blankness. And when you expect threat, you find it — or rather, you find threat-signals in places that don't actually contain threat, because your system has been tuned to be exquisitely sensitive to any cue that looks like what it learned to fear.

This is why adults who grew up in emotionally neglectful homes often describe a constant, low-level feeling of wrongness — a pervasive sense that something is about to go wrong, that they are somehow unsafe, that they need to be careful, even in circumstances that are objectively benign. Their neuroceptive system is running old code. It is still scanning for the absence of attunement. And it keeps finding it, or near-enough facsimiles of it, everywhere.


The Three States: A Hierarchy of Survival

Polyvagal theory describes three broad states that the nervous system moves through in response to safety and threat. Understanding these states is essential for understanding why childhood emotional neglect produces the specific patterns it produces.

At the top of the hierarchy is what Porges calls the ventral vagal state — named for the ventral vagal complex, the part of the vagus nerve that governs the social engagement system. When you are in ventral vagal activation, you feel safe. You can make easy eye contact. Your voice has prosody — natural variation and warmth. Your facial muscles are mobile and expressive. You can listen without distraction, connect without effort, think clearly, and tolerate a range of emotions without being overwhelmed. This is the state of genuine rest. Of connection. Of being at home in your own skin.

Below that, in the hierarchy of survival, is the sympathetic activation state — the familiar fight-or-flight response. When the neuroceptive system detects threat that the social engagement system cannot resolve through connection, the sympathetic branch activates. Heart rate climbs. Muscles ready for action. Attention narrows. Digestion pauses. You are being prepared to fight or flee. This state is designed to be short-lived — to spike in the face of danger and then return to calm once the danger has passed. When it works as designed, you flush with stress hormones, take the necessary action, discharge the energy through movement, and come back down.

Below that — and this is where it gets strange — is the dorsal vagal state, the most primitive of the three. When the neuroceptive system determines that neither fight nor flight is viable — that the threat is inescapable and that action is impossible — the oldest part of the vagus nerve takes over. And it shuts things down. Heart rate drops. Breathing shallows. The person freezes, collapses, dissociates, or goes numb. This is the physiological mechanism behind playing dead. Animals do it when caught. Humans do it when the danger is relational — when the threat is coming from the very person they depend on for survival, and there is literally no escape.

The ladder matters because these states aren't arbitrary. They are a hierarchy — a series of fallback positions, each more ancient and more desperate than the last, each deployed when the one above has failed. You try connection first. If connection fails, you mobilise. If mobilisation fails, you collapse.

Now imagine a child whose primary experience of the social engagement system — whose primary experience of reaching for connection — is that it consistently fails. Not through violence, but through blankness. Through repeated non-response. Through the thousand daily moments in which they looked for a face and found nothing.

What does that child learn? They learn that the top of the hierarchy doesn't work. They learn, not in words but in the body's most fundamental language, that connection doesn't resolve threat. And so they spend more time than they should in the states below.


The Chronically Primed System

The window of tolerance is a concept developed by psychiatrist Dan Siegel to describe the zone in which the nervous system is able to function optimally. Not switched off, not overactivated — awake, engaged, capable of handling incoming experience without being overwhelmed. Within the window, you can feel your feelings without being capsized by them. You can think and feel simultaneously. You can respond rather than react.

The window is not a fixed size. It varies from person to person, and it varies in the same person across states. Stress narrows it. Rest widens it. And — crucially — early relational experiences shape its baseline width.

A child who has received consistent co-regulation develops a relatively wide window. They have been brought to calm so many times that calm is, for them, an accessible state. Their nervous system knows how to return to regulation because it has been returned there thousands of times from the outside before it learned to manage the journey alone. Stressful things still push them toward the edges. But the edges are far enough away that they rarely tip over into full dysregulation.

A child who has not received this — whose baseline experience was the unmet alarm, the unacknowledged distress, the moment of needing something and finding nothing — develops a narrower window. Their nervous system has fewer stored experiences of return-to-calm. It has calibrated for a world in which distress is not reliably resolved. And so it holds itself at a higher ambient level of readiness, just in case. The alarm is set lighter. The trigger is more sensitive.

What this looks like in an adult is difficult to describe from the outside, because it often doesn't look like anything. The adult goes to work, manages their relationships, holds things together. But internally, the nervous system is running hot. Small triggers produce large responses that seem disproportionate. A minor conflict at work produces the cortisol spike of a genuine emergency. A friend who doesn't reply quickly produces a wash of fear that the conscious mind knows is irrational but that the body insists on anyway. The person knows, intellectually, that they are safe. The knowledge sits there, useless, while the nervous system goes on doing what it was calibrated to do.

The three characteristic patterns that emerge from this are worth examining carefully, because they look very different from each other on the surface while sharing the same underlying architecture.

Hypervigilance is the sympathetic-dominant pattern. The nervous system stays in a state of low-grade readiness, scanning constantly for threat. The person is easily startled. They read the room wherever they go. They anticipate conflict before it arrives. They over-analyse social interactions. They have a hard time relaxing, and on the occasions when they do relax, something else happens: the sudden absence of vigilance feels dangerous, as though letting their guard down has made them vulnerable. They may sleep badly, because sleep requires a degree of dorsal-vagal shutdown that their sympathetic system keeps interrupting. They are often described as anxious, which is accurate, but "anxious" undersells the physiological reality — this is a system that cannot come down from alert, not a mind that's worrying too much.

Freeze is the dorsal-vagal pattern. When overwhelm arrives and the sympathetic system's mobilised energy finds nowhere to go — when there's no appropriate fight, no available flight — the system drops into shutdown. This looks like depression, or numbness, or the strange blankness of sitting in front of a task that's urgent and being completely unable to begin it. Freeze is often confused with laziness, with lack of motivation, with not caring. It is none of these things. It is a nervous system that has defaulted to its oldest survival strategy because nothing else was available. The person isn't choosing inaction. The system has chosen it for them.

Fawn is the most socially legible pattern and the most specifically relational. Fawn is what happens when a child's neuroceptive system learns that the route to safety is not through fighting, not through fleeing, and not through collapse — but through attentiveness to the emotional state of the other person. If the caregiver is unpredictable, you learn to read them. You learn to become what they need you to be. You suppress your own signals — your own needs, your own distress, your own presence — in order to manage theirs. And this becomes automatic. The fawn response in adulthood produces people who are extraordinarily attuned to others and almost entirely disconnected from themselves. They can read a room like a professional. They know what everyone else needs. They have very little idea what they need.

None of these are character flaws. All of them are adaptations. The body found a strategy that kept it alive, or at least kept it safe in the specific environment it grew up in. The problem is that the strategy was built for that environment — for that specific house, that specific childhood, those specific caregivers — and the body didn't get the memo that the environment changed.


The Strange Problem of Not Knowing What You Feel

There is one more dimension to this that is worth its own section, because it's often the least visible and the most consequential. It has to do with interoception.

Interoception is the body's sense of itself — the ongoing stream of signals from internal organs, muscles, and viscera that tells the brain about hunger, fullness, warmth, cold, pain, fatigue, emotional activation, and hundreds of other states. It is sometimes called the sixth sense, and in terms of its importance to daily functioning, it may be the most important sense there is. Without reliable interoceptive signals, you have difficulty knowing when you're hungry, when you're tired, when you're anxious, when you're excited, when you're in pain, and when you've reached your limit. You lose, in a very real sense, access to the information that would let you take care of yourself.

A 2023 meta-analysis published in Borderline Personality Disorder and Emotion Dysregulation, drawing on 17 studies with more than 3,700 participants, found that emotional maltreatment in childhood — including neglect — was strongly linked to reduced body trust. Not necessarily impaired interoceptive accuracy (the signals were often there) but something subtler and stranger: a habitual non-attendance to those signals. A learned pattern of disregarding what the body was saying.

This makes precise sense within the polyvagal framework. If you grew up in a household where your emotional signals were consistently not met — where you felt distress and that distress produced no response, or worse, produced a negative response — then your interoceptive signals became associated with futility. Feeling something led nowhere. Expressing what you felt led nowhere or led to trouble. And so, very gradually, you learned to turn down the volume. Not through conscious choice, but because the organism discovered that the signals weren't useful. They weren't information anymore. They were just noise.

This shows up in adults in ways that can be genuinely bewildering. They struggle to identify emotions — not because they don't have them, but because the emotions don't come labelled. They arrive as vague physical sensations: tightness in the chest, a hollow feeling in the stomach, heat in the face. Alexithymia, the clinical term for difficulty identifying and describing one's own emotions, is substantially more common in adults with histories of emotional neglect. The person who responds to "how do you feel about that?" with "I don't know — I can't really tell" is not being evasive. They are reporting accurately on a genuine sensory deficit.

More practically: without reliable body signals, self-care becomes extraordinarily difficult. You don't know when to eat because hunger arrives either as a crisis or not at all. You don't know when to rest because fatigue doesn't register clearly. You don't know when a relationship is bad for you because the distress signals don't accumulate into anything legible until you're already in trouble. The body is trying to tell you things. The volume has just been turned down too low to hear.

There is also something harder to pin down, but reported consistently by people who have done substantial healing work in this area: a quality of estrangement from their own physical existence. A sense of watching themselves from a slight distance. Of being present but not quite inhabiting the body they're in. This is the dorsal-vagal element — the oldest of the three survival strategies — expressing itself not as acute shutdown but as a chronic, low-level dissociation. Not dramatic. Not debilitating. Just: life experienced as slightly removed from oneself, as if through thin glass.


What It Looks Like from the Inside

It might be useful, before talking about repair, to describe what this constellation of effects actually looks like in the day-to-day experience of an adult who carries it. Because from the outside, the adult often appears functional. More than functional, sometimes. They can be extraordinarily capable people, because some of the survival strategies produce behaviours that look a lot like virtues.

The hypervigilant adult is often described as perceptive, sensitive, conscientious. They read situations well and anticipate problems. The fawning adult is often experienced as deeply empathic, generous, accommodating. The freeze-prone adult may have developed elaborate intellectual and creative lives that function as regulated spaces — the inner life organised and rich even when the external one feels impossible to navigate.

But from the inside, there is a persistent wrongness that is very hard to account for. A feeling of never quite landing. Of relationships that feel good from a distance and uncomfortable up close. Of being, somehow, always slightly in preparation for something that never arrives. Of working hard, achieving things, and finding that the achievement lands without joy — not because the person is ungrateful, but because the nervous system's appraisal of safety hasn't changed, and the ventral vagal state in which genuine satisfaction lives is hard to reach and harder to stay in.

There is often a specific quality of loneliness that is very difficult to describe to people who haven't experienced it. Not the loneliness of being alone — the adult can often fill their time fine, and may prefer it. But the loneliness of being with others and still not quite arriving. Of watching connection happen around them, or even participating in it, with some dim awareness that something they should be feeling isn't coming through. The social engagement system is there. But the conditions under which it learned to function — the specific cues of genuine safety, of being truly seen and met — were established in a particular relational environment, and they don't automatically transfer to the adult world.


What Nervous System Repair Actually Looks Like

Here is the thing that is both hopeful and demanding: the nervous system is plastic. Not infinitely, not easily, but genuinely. It can change. The same capacity for calibration that made it so responsive to the original relational environment makes it responsive to new relational environments too. What was learned through relationship can be unlearned — or more precisely, supplemented — through relationship. Not through understanding alone. Through experience.

This is the point where conventional therapy often falls short. Insight, however profound, doesn't directly change the body. You can understand perfectly well that your hypervigilance is a childhood adaptation, that your fawn response is a survival strategy, that your numbness is dorsal-vagal shutdown — and still wake at 3am with your heart hammering, still lose yourself in the service of someone else's needs, still sit in front of urgent tasks unable to begin. The understanding is true. But it lives in the cortex, the part of the brain capable of narrative. The dysregulation lives deeper — in the brainstem, in the autonomic system, in the body's own memory. And the body doesn't speak in words.

This is why the most effective approaches to nervous system repair work directly with the body rather than through it. Somatic experiencing, developed by trauma specialist Peter Levine, works with the physical sensations and impulses that trauma leaves in the body — the fight that couldn't happen, the flight that was blocked — and helps them complete and discharge. EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing) uses bilateral stimulation to help the nervous system process memories it couldn't integrate at the time of experience. Both approaches rest on the polyvagal understanding that the nervous system needs to experience regulation before it can integrate anything — that the window of tolerance has to be established first, not as an afterthought.

The concept of pendulation captures something essential. In somatic approaches, pendulation means gently oscillating between activation and regulation, between the edge of distress and a resource of calm, over and over and over. Not plunging into the distress and swimming around. Not avoiding the distress. Moving toward it a little, feeling it, moving away, feeling the calm. The nervous system, encountering this pattern repeatedly, begins to learn something new: that activation doesn't mean catastrophe. That the distress rises and falls. That it is survivable. That calm exists on the other side of it, and is consistently accessible.

This sounds simple. In practice it is among the most demanding things a person can undertake, because it requires sitting with sensations that the system has spent decades organising itself to avoid. The body doesn't give up its strategies easily. They worked. They kept the person alive and functional in an environment that required them. And the body treats their absence as danger.

What the research is increasingly clear on is that significant, durable change requires what is sometimes called a corrective relational experience — the actual experience, over time, of being with another person in a way that feels genuinely safe. This can happen in therapy with a skilled, regulated therapist whose own nervous system provides the co-regulation that was missing early on. It can happen in significant adult relationships, though it requires a quality of presence that not all relationships can provide. The mechanism is the same as the original developmental process: another regulated nervous system, consistently available, teaching the dysregulated one what safety feels like in the body.

Titration matters here. You cannot rush the nervous system. A system primed for threat treats too much too fast as confirmation of its worst assumptions — the intensity itself becomes a signal that something is wrong. Small doses. Gentle approach. Extended time. The window of tolerance has to be gradually widened from the inside, not forced open from the outside.

There are also specific practices that support this process, many of which work by directly stimulating the vagus nerve. Slow diaphragmatic breathing — particularly with a longer exhalation than inhalation — activates the parasympathetic system and can, with repetition, shift the baseline tone of the nervous system toward greater flexibility. Cold water on the face activates the dive reflex, a vagally-mediated response that slows the heart. Humming, singing, and gargling tone the muscles attached to the vagus nerve and stimulate it through vibration. None of these are cures. All of them are practices — things that, done consistently over time, begin to influence the system's resting state in the direction of greater flexibility and accessibility.

Tracking sensation — learning to notice what the body is actually saying, without immediately interpreting or suppressing it — is often the foundation of everything else. For the person whose interoceptive signals have been turned down by years of non-attendance, this can feel bizarre at first. The signals are faint. The habit of not listening is deeply grooved. But sensation-tracking can be learned, slowly, the same way any skill is learned: by practicing it in small, low-stakes moments. Not in the middle of a crisis, but in the ordinary textures of a quiet afternoon. What does my chest feel like right now? Not "what am I feeling," which requires interpretation — just: what is the physical sensation? Warmth or coolness? Tight or loose? Moving or still?

This is the beginning of learning to read your own body again. It is, in a meaningful sense, the beginning of coming home.


The Political Point

There is something worth pausing on before closing, which is about what all this tells us about the nature of care.

The picture that polyvagal theory paints is one in which human beings are, from the very first moments of life, fundamentally relational. Not just psychologically — not just in the soft sense that connection matters — but physiologically. The capacity to feel safe is built through relationship. It cannot be bootstrapped alone. It requires another nervous system, another person, another presence that is genuinely available and genuinely attuned.

Childhood emotional neglect tends to be understood, when it's understood at all, as a psychological wound. An emotional deficit. Something that leaves people feeling unloved or unimportant. And it does those things. But it does something more fundamental too: it alters the hardware. Not just the feelings a person carries, but the way their nervous system processes experience, detects threat, and accesses calm. The wound is physiological before it is psychological. The repair has to match.

This matters for how we understand the adults who carry it. The hypervigilance is not a personality flaw. The freeze is not laziness. The fawn is not weakness. The disconnection from one's own body is not emotional immaturity. These are the intelligent, adaptive responses of a nervous system that learned what it learned in the only school available to it, and applied those lessons faithfully across decades. The body did exactly what it was supposed to do. The problem was not the body. The problem was what the body was given to work with.

And if that's true, then the path forward is not about willpower, or discipline, or thinking harder about things. It is about giving the body new information, slowly, in a language it actually speaks. Not the language of words. The language of safety. The language of another regulated presence, close enough that the nervous system can feel it. The language of being seen — finally, genuinely, by someone who stays.

That baby in the room learned something real: that aloneness was the default, that distress went unmet, that the reaching-out of the social engagement system produced nothing. The learning was accurate. The environment was what it was.

But the nervous system is not a record. It is a living system. And living systems, given the right conditions, update.
