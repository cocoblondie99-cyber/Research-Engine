The House With the Tripped Breaker

You probably know the feeling. A jaw that won't unclench even after a good night's sleep. Shoulders that seem to have migrated upward toward your ears and set up permanent residence there. A low-grade ache in the hips that no amount of stretching quite touches, a constriction across the chest that you've learned to call baseline. You've adapted around these things. You assume it's posture, or stress, or just getting older. But consider the possibility that this tension has a biography.

For people who grew up in emotionally neglected households — homes where the emotional needs of children were consistently unmet, not through cruelty but through absence, through distraction, through a parent's own unprocessed wounds — something specific happened in the body. Not just in the mind. The feelings that had nowhere safe to go found somewhere else to go instead. They went into muscle, into fascia, into the nervous system's wiring. They went, in the phrase the psychiatrist Wilhelm Reich coined nearly a century ago, into armor.

Reich was eccentric, controversial, and eventually discredited for reasons unrelated to his clinical observations. But his insight that the body literally hardens around unprocessed emotional experience — that the muscle braces where the self once braced against pain — has been quietly confirmed by decades of subsequent neuroscience and somatic psychology. The armor isn't metaphorical. It's measurable. It's in the elevated baseline tone of your trapezius muscles, the chronic contraction of your psoas, the tight capsule of your jaw. And for people whose earliest environment taught them that feelings were dangerous or unwelcome, it started forming very early indeed.

Think of the nervous system as a circuit breaker. When the emotional current flowing through a child's body is too much — when the feelings are real but there's nowhere for them to land, no co-regulation, no parent who meets the distress and helps discharge it — the system trips. The current has to go somewhere. Rather than flowing through and dissipating, it gets routed into the muscles, which brace against it, creating a kind of biological dam. The house keeps running. You function. You get through days and years and decades. But somewhere in the walls there's a background hum of voltage that was never discharged. And certain rooms have been dark for so long you've stopped noticing.

This is the territory we're exploring: why tension gets physically trapped, the specific mechanisms by which it does so, and what it takes to begin releasing it. With a particular focus on one of the stranger obstacles to that release — the tendency, characteristic of a certain type of mind, to study the body from a distance rather than actually inhabit it.

---

The psoas muscle is perhaps the most important muscle most people have never heard of. It runs deep — too deep to feel directly — connecting your lumbar spine to your femur, threading through your pelvis in a way that makes it fundamental to almost every movement you make. Stand up, and your psoas is working. Sit, and it's shortened. Walk, and it's cycling. But its real significance isn't mechanical. It's neurological.

The psoas is the body's primary fright-or-flight contractor. When the amygdala detects threat and the sympathetic nervous system fires, one of the first things that happens is a deep contraction of the psoas — the body curls forward protectively, preparing to run or brace. Under normal circumstances, once the threat passes, the psoas releases. But in people who grew up under conditions of chronic, low-grade emotional threat — the unpredictability of an unavailable parent, the constant background vigilance of a child who has learned that connection is unreliable — the psoas rarely gets to fully release. It stays shortened, slightly contracted, chronically ready. Over years, this becomes structural. The muscle and its surrounding fascia lose elasticity. The contraction becomes the body's new resting state.

This matters for several reasons beyond the obvious one of lower back pain, though lower back pain is certainly common in people with this history. The psoas shares fascial continuity with the diaphragm — the membrane that controls breathing. A chronically contracted psoas pulls on the diaphragm, compresses the breath, and contributes to the shallow chest-breathing that both signals and perpetuates a nervous system that doesn't feel safe. Breathing and psoas tension form a feedback loop: the muscle that braces in fear also constricts the breath that would help regulate the fear. The body, trying to protect itself, makes it harder to come back down.

This pattern — bracing, armoring, hypertonic muscle — isn't limited to the psoas. It shows up wherever in the body a person has learned to hold. The jaw clenches against words not spoken, frustration not expressed, the constant micro-effort of keeping things back. The shoulders brace against a world that felt unreliable. The belly tightens against feelings that felt unsafe to have. Over time, these habitual contractions become the baseline — the person can no longer feel them as contractions because they've become the body's ordinary state. The armoring is now invisible to the person wearing it.

Alongside this is a phenomenon called interoceptive dissociation — a dimming of the body's own internal reporting system. Interoception is the sense of what's happening inside you: hunger, fatigue, emotional sensation as physical feeling. Under chronic stress, the nervous system learns to turn this signal down. Feeling less is safer when feelings carry no relief. But this creates an unusual problem: the person whose body is most in need of attention is often the person least able to feel it. The dashboard has gone dark not because nothing is happening — but because the system learned, long ago, that looking at the dashboard didn't help.

---

Here is where David Berceli's TRE — Tension and Trauma Releasing Exercises — becomes interesting.

Berceli, a trauma therapist who has worked in conflict zones, noticed something that most humans have observed in animals but never thought to apply to themselves. When a prey animal escapes a predator, it doesn't just trot away. It trembles. The whole body shakes involuntarily for a minute or two — a full-system discharge of the accumulated survival energy — and then it resumes grazing as though nothing happened. The nervous system has completed the threat cycle. The cortisol and adrenaline are metabolized, the muscles that braced are allowed to release, and the animal returns to baseline. No rumination. No post-traumatic stress. Just: shake and continue.

Humans have this mechanism. It's neurogenic tremoring, and it's entirely involuntary — the same trembling you might notice in your legs after a near-accident, or in your hands after a frightening confrontation. The shaking isn't weakness. It's the nervous system doing exactly what it's designed to do: completing the stress cycle and discharging the accumulated charge. But humans, unlike gazelles, have been culturally taught to suppress this response. We are told to pull ourselves together. We learn that shaking is embarrassing, that trembling signals loss of control. We override the reflex, consistently, every time it arises.

The result, across years and decades, is accumulation. The discharge never happens, so the tension accretes. TRE works by deliberately inducing neurogenic tremors through a series of exercises that fatigue the leg muscles and then allow the body's tremoring reflex to activate. The person doesn't cause the shaking — they allow it. The body does the work it's been waiting to do. For many people, particularly those with a long history of chronic body armoring, the first session produces an uncanny sense of unfamiliar ease — not relaxation exactly, but a kind of unlocking that feels strange precisely because the lock had been there so long.

Other active practices work through adjacent mechanisms. Somatic movement — slow, exploratory movement that invites sensation rather than pushing through it — gradually reawakens the body's proprioceptive and interoceptive reporting. Expressive movement and shaking practices (simple, deliberate shaking of limbs) work on similar principles to TRE. Yoga nidra — a guided practice of deep relaxation with sustained body awareness — functions as a bridge between the body and the mind's observer, providing something the nervous system hasn't had much of: the experience of being in the body while nothing threatening is happening.

The passive practices operate by creating conditions under which the body can release rather than bracing through any effort at release. Yin yoga holds positions for three to five minutes, allowing the connective tissue and fascia — which respond to time rather than force — to slowly surrender. Restorative yoga does something similar with the added element of full support: the body is held by bolsters and blankets and has nothing it needs to do. Float therapy removes gravity, temperature sensation, and visual input, producing a state in which the nervous system has nothing to brace against and often, for the first time in years, gently unbraces. Weighted blankets work differently — applying deep pressure touch, which activates the parasympathetic nervous system through mechanoreceptors, reducing the body's sense of background alert.

Progressive muscle relaxation, developed by Edmund Jacobson in the 1920s, exploits a simple physiological fact: a muscle that has been deliberately tensed and then released is more relaxed than one that was never tensed at all. The systematic tensing and releasing of muscle groups teaches the nervous system what release actually feels like — useful, particularly, for people who have held tension so long they've lost the contrast.

Self-massage deserves particular attention, partly because it's accessible and partly because touch itself carries a distinctive neurological significance for those whose early attachment was deficient. The jaw is an excellent starting point: hold the masseter (the muscle that runs from cheekbone to jawline) between thumb and fingers, apply gentle sustained pressure, and feel what's there. Most people find it remarkable. The hip flexors can be addressed with a supported bridge position and patient waiting, or with focused pressure along the psoas attachment points. The neck and shoulder work is more familiar but benefits from slow deliberateness — not rolling, but resting, finding the tightest points and simply staying with them until they soften.

---

All of this exists in the context of a particular difficulty that will resonate with some readers more than others.

The Enneagram Type 5 is characterised by a fundamental retreat into the mind. The world is experienced primarily through observation, analysis, and the elaboration of concepts. The 5 is not unaware of the body — they can describe its sensations in considerable detail, map its anatomical structure, theorize about its relationship to emotion. What they have difficulty doing is inhabiting it. There is a gap, often profound, between cognition and felt experience, and this gap is not a failure of intelligence or curiosity but a deeply adaptive response to an early environment in which emotional presence felt dangerous or unwanted.

The child who learned that needing was not met, that emotional expression generated withdrawal or overwhelm in the caregiver, that the safest place was inside the mind where nothing could be demanded of you — that child learned to observe the body from a safe distance. To map it rather than live in it. This becomes so habitual that even in adulthood, attempts to connect with the body tend to become cognitive projects. The 5 who reads about somatic practices acquires a detailed and accurate model of how somatic practices work, and uses this model to conduct observations of their own body with the same detached interest they'd apply to any other phenomenon. The tremors are noticed and classified. The sensation in the psoas is identified and cross-referenced. And the body, somehow, stays largely unchanged.

This isn't failure. It's a deeply entrenched adaptation encountering a method that requires it to do the very thing it was designed to prevent. The standard somatic instruction — "notice what you're feeling in your body" — immediately activates the observer stance that is the 5's primary defense. Noticing becomes another cognitive task, and the task is completed without any actual felt shift.

The way around this is not through more careful observation but through less of it. The practices that work best for this particular configuration tend to be ones that either bypass the observer entirely or make observation temporarily impossible.

TRE is useful precisely because the tremoring is involuntary. The 5 doesn't have to feel the tremors the right way or understand them correctly — the body simply does something the mind did not authorise, and this involuntary element is, paradoxically, the point. There is no observation-and-analysis loop to run because the process doesn't require the observer's participation.

Yoga nidra and float therapy work by suspending the sense that there is anything to do. Float particularly — deprived of the external inputs the mind can busy itself with, removed from gravity, held at body temperature in the dark — offers the 5 the unusual experience of having nothing to monitor or manage. The protective observer has nothing to observe. Into this space, occasionally, comes genuine felt experience. It can be startling.

The broader principle is: go directly to movement or surrender before the mind has time to install itself as the mediator. Shake before you think about shaking. Float before you think about what floating is supposed to do. Let the psoas release before you've fully theorised the mechanism of the release.

The question at the centre of this is one that can be asked intellectually or actually: What does it mean to inhabit a body you were never taught to trust?

Asking it intellectually is useful. The Enneagram 5 can produce a thoughtful, even moving, essay on the subject. But the question only becomes real at the moment of actual inhabitation — not understanding the concept of trusting the body, but feeling, briefly, for the first time, that the body is somewhere you can be.

---

That moment, when it comes, tends to arrive quietly and without announcement. Not as an insight but as a settling. The shoulders come down. The breath finds more room. The background hum in the walls — the voltage that's been there since childhood, the current that never discharged — begins, slowly, to move.

The circuit breaker in the panel does not repair itself in a single session. This is years-long work, and it is not linear. The body releases, and then protects again; the nervous system opens, and then braces against the openness. But there is a difference between a system that has never experienced release and one that has — however briefly — felt what it's like to let the current move. The body remembers. Even the body that spent decades not being trusted.

Reaching into the panel isn't about understanding the wiring. It's about, again and again, finding the lever. Letting something shift that had been locked. And slowly, unexpectedly, finding that some of the dark rooms have light in them after all.
