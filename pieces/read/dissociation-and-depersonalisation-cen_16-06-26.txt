The Solution That Stayed

Imagine a child who learns early that raising their hand in a classroom where no teacher ever looks up is pointless. After a while, they stop raising it. After a while longer, they stop being aware they ever wanted to. The hand stays down. The wanting quiets. And what arrives in its place is something that looks, from the outside, like a well-behaved child — and feels, from the inside, like nothing very much at all.

This is not a metaphor for inattention or poor teaching. It is a description of what happens inside a nervous system that grows up in a home where emotional signals are sent and never returned. Millions of adults are living this way right now — not quite feeling what they know they should feel, not quite present in their own bodies, moving through their days in a quiet, baffling fog. Many of them have spent years wondering what is wrong with them. Most of them do not have a word for what happened.

The word is dissociation. And the first, most important thing to understand about it is that it was never a mistake.


What emotional neglect actually does to the internal world

Childhood emotional neglect — CEN, in the clinical shorthand — is not the presence of something harmful. It is the absence of something necessary. A caregiver who shouts is at least responding. A caregiver who is emotionally blank, preoccupied, dismissive, or simply not there in the felt sense — even while physically present — gives the child's internal signals nowhere to go.

Babies and young children are not self-contained emotional units. They experience a feeling — hunger, fear, delight, discomfort — and they broadcast it, loudly and physically. What they are waiting for, what they are biologically wired to expect, is a response that mirrors the feeling back in a regulated form. The caregiver's face and voice and body say, in effect: I see what you feel. It is real. And here is what to do with it. This is attunement, and it is the mechanism by which emotional experience gets processed rather than just felt.

When attunement does not reliably come — when the signal is sent and nothing returns — the child's emotional world becomes a place of strange pressure with no release valve. Feelings accumulate without being metabolised. The internal world fills up, and there is no one to help empty it.

This is where dissociation begins. Not as a disorder. Not as a failure. As a solution.


The oldest circuit in the building

The human nervous system has three broad response modes, and understanding them is the key to understanding why emotional neglect produces the particular symptoms it does.

The first is social engagement — the parasympathetic state of genuine safety, where we can connect, think clearly, and feel our feelings without being overwhelmed by them. This is the nervous system at its most sophisticated.

The second is fight-or-flight — the sympathetic activation that mobilises the body for action in response to threat. Heart rate up, muscles primed, awareness narrowed to the danger.

The third is something older and quieter: the dorsal vagal response. This is the evolutionary baseline, the circuit that predates the social nervous system by hundreds of millions of years. It is what a lizard does when cornered with no escape — it goes still. It plays dead. It shuts down. It becomes, in some essential sense, less present than before.

In acute danger, this response is a brilliant piece of biological engineering. Immobility can make you invisible. Shutdown reduces pain. Stillness conserves energy.

But here is the problem for children in emotionally neglectful homes: the dorsal vagal system activates not only in response to physical danger, but in response to any situation where neither fight nor flight is available — and where the threat is chronic. A child who is frightened of a caregiver can run away. A child who is in the presence of a caregiver who simply does not respond emotionally cannot fight the absence or flee from the emptiness. There is no action to take. And so the oldest circuit in the building begins to fire.

Not once. Not occasionally. Chronically. The shutdown response that was designed as a temporary emergency measure becomes the nervous system's default resting state. The house stops registering temperature. Not because it broke down, but because the thermostat decided — at some level far below language or decision — that there was no point measuring a room that would never be heated anyway.


What this looks like from the inside

The symptoms of chronic dorsal vagal activation look, from the outside, like passivity or apathy. From the inside, they feel like something far stranger: a kind of thickness in the air between oneself and one's own experience.

People with CEN routinely describe watching their own life from a mild distance. Knowing they should be excited about something and registering a flat space where the excitement was supposed to be. Going through the motions of emotions that feel, as they produce them, slightly performed. Struggling to remember what they felt during experiences that were, by all external measures, significant.

Some people describe it as a glass wall between themselves and their feelings. Others describe watching themselves from slightly above and behind, as if they were a camera operator filming their own life. The clinical term for this second experience is depersonalisation — the sense that the self is not quite real, or is not quite one's own.

Depersonalisation sits at the extreme end of a spectrum, and it is important to understand that the spectrum is very wide. On one end: a mild background numbness, a tendency to feel less than others seem to feel, a vague sense of going through motions. On the other end: full dissociative episodes, where a person may have no clear sense of who they are, where they are, or how much time has passed. In between: intermittent unreality, emotional blunting punctuated by brief overwhelming floods, a persistent sense of watching rather than participating.

The research on this is stark: emotional neglect — the absence of attunement — shows a stronger association with dissociative symptoms than any other form of childhood maltreatment, including physical abuse. Absence, paradoxically, is more dissociation-inducing than presence. This makes sense when you understand the mechanism. Abuse produces fear, anger, grief — the fight-or-flight system is activated, loudly and clearly. Neglect produces the situation where neither fight nor flight is possible, where there is nothing to respond to, and so the dorsal vagal system fires. It is the difference between a loud alarm and a building that simply stops all its systems and goes quiet.


The way the system defends itself

One of the strangest features of dissociation as a chronic state is that it becomes invisible to the person inside it. This is not coincidental. It is part of how the system works.

When a nervous system learns that internal signals lead nowhere — that hunger and fear and longing and delight are all produced, sent, and not returned — it begins, gradually, to reduce the production of those signals. Not entirely. But to a level that is manageable. Bearable. That does not generate the particular pain of sending and being met with silence.

The awareness of wanting becomes suppressed along with the wanting. The awareness of feeling becomes suppressed along with the feeling. What remains is a functional person who does not quite know that anything is missing, because the very apparatus that would notice the gap has been turned down.

This is why many people with CEN do not identify themselves as having a difficult childhood. Nothing bad happened. Meals appeared. The lights worked. They went to school. What they do not have easy access to is the felt sense of what it was like to be a child in that house, and they often have very little sense of what emotional warmth and attunement feel like, because they do not have a comparison.

They find out something was missing, usually, when they try to access feelings they cannot find. When they watch a film that should move them and notice something hollow in the place where the emotion was supposed to be. When they struggle to feel real love for people they know they love. When they arrive at the therapist's office and, asked how they feel, genuinely do not know.


The counterintuitive discovery at the start of recovery

Here is the piece of information that most people find the most confusing, and that is also the most important.

When someone with CEN begins to do recovery work — therapy, somatic practice, emotional literacy work, anything that starts to thaw the dissociated state — the first thing to return is not pleasant. It is not warmth, or joy, or connection. What comes back first is grief. Anger. Longing. The painful feelings.

This feels, to many people, like getting worse. They came to therapy to feel better. They have begun to feel worse. The obvious conclusion is that the therapy is not working, or is working in the wrong direction, or is actively harmful.

The actual explanation is something like the opposite. The dissociative system suppressed all affect together. Not just the painful feelings — all of them. The shutdown that protected against the pain of unmet emotional signals also muffled joy, curiosity, warmth, pleasure, and meaning. It is a blunt instrument. It does not selectively remove suffering while leaving satisfaction intact. It turns the volume down on the entire interior world.

When the volume begins to come back up, the painful feelings are louder, and they were there first, and they have been waiting. The grief for the attunement that never came is enormous — the size of a whole childhood of unmet reaching. The anger is the anger that a child could never safely express, compressed for decades. These feelings arrive before the pleasant ones because they were the primary signal that the dissociative system was responding to. They are the most pressurised.

Recovery workers sometimes describe this as defrosting. The freezer has been on for so long that everything in it is solid. When the temperature begins to rise, the things that melt first are the things that were most liquid before they froze — the intense feelings, the grief, the longing. The warmth and the pleasure come later, once the system begins to trust that feeling is survivable.

What makes this almost unbearably counterintuitive is that the first sign of healing looks, and feels, like breakdown.


What reconnection actually requires

The recovery from chronic dissociation is not, despite what the word recovery might imply, a matter of getting back to a former state. For most people with CEN, there was no former state of rich emotional presence to return to. The nervous system was shaped from early enough that this is more like a first time than a return.

This distinction matters practically. The approach cannot be top-down — it cannot be understanding the concept of feelings and then deciding to have them. The dorsal vagal system does not respond to intellectual instruction. You cannot think your way out of a physiological shutdown any more than you can think your way out of a faint.

What works, instead, is a slow accumulation of embodied evidence that the internal world is safe to inhabit. The evidence comes from somatic practice — the gentle, incremental work of learning to notice what is happening in the body without immediately retreating from it. A breath. A sensation in the chest. The recognition that a feeling is passing through without causing catastrophe.

It comes from relational experience — the experience of sending an emotional signal and having it returned. This can happen in therapy, in close friendships, in a good partner relationship. The nervous system is, at its core, a social organ. It learned its current operating parameters in relationship. It is most efficiently re-educated in relationship.

And it requires an unusual form of patience: the patience to tolerate discomfort that feels like getting worse as evidence of getting better. To feel the grief and recognise it as the beginning of feeling, rather than further proof that something is broken.

The thermostat does not restart all at once. It begins, tentatively, to register the temperature. To report a reading and discover that the report is received. To sense that the house, this time, might be willing to respond. And then, gradually, to do what thermostats are built for — to read what is actually happening in the room, accurately, without pre-emptively switching off.

This is what the nervous system is trying to learn. Not to be more emotional. Not to perform what it has been told it should feel. But to discover, with genuine surprise, that the reaching can go somewhere now — and that what comes back, when it does, is the beginning of being present in a life that has been waiting.
