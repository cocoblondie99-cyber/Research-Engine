The Story That Was Never Told

There is a problem at the very heart of healing from childhood emotional neglect, and it shows up the moment you walk into a therapist's office and sit down on the couch.

Talk therapy — the whole grand tradition of it, from Freud's consulting room to the modern CBT worksheet — runs on narrative. You are invited to tell what happened. The therapist listens for the event, the rupture, the moment things went wrong, and together you work on the meaning you made of it. This is a beautifully effective machine when there is a story to tell. A car crash. A betrayal. A loss. A cruelty done to you that you can name, date, and describe.

But emotional neglect leaves no story. That is its entire signature. Nothing happened. No blow was struck, no door was slammed, no terrible single afternoon can be circled on the calendar. What happened to the emotionally neglected child is an absence — the absence of a parent who noticed the inner weather, who asked what was wrong and waited for an answer, who reflected feelings back until the child learned to recognise their own. And you cannot narrate an absence. You cannot tell the story of the thing that didn't occur. People who grew up this way often arrive in therapy saying some version of: "I had a fine childhood. Nothing bad happened. I don't know why I feel like this." They are not in denial. They are telling the literal truth. The damage was done in the negative space, in everything that was supposed to be there and wasn't.

So here is the mismatch the rest of this piece is about. The default tool of psychological healing presupposes an event. The defining feature of neglect is that there was no event — only a long, quiet, formative nothing. Which raises a genuinely strange question: if you cannot get at the wound through words, how do you get at it at all?

One increasingly compelling answer is that you go in through the body instead.

The Animals That Shake It Off

To understand why, it helps to meet the person who built the most influential body-based approach to trauma, and the observation that started him off.

Peter Levine, the founder of a method called Somatic Experiencing, spent years puzzling over a curious fact about wild animals. Prey animals — gazelles, rabbits, impala — live lives saturated with mortal threat. A gazelle may be chased to within an inch of death by a cheetah several times a week. By every logic of human psychology, these animals ought to be quivering wrecks, riddled with what we'd call post-traumatic stress. And yet they are not. They graze calmly minutes after a near-fatal sprint.

Levine watched what they actually do in the moments after a threat passes, and noticed something most of us have seen without registering its importance. The animal shakes. It trembles, its legs judder, it breathes in great heaving gusts — and then, the discharge complete, it simply gets up and walks back to the herd as if nothing had happened. The terror moves through the body and out. The system resets to zero.

His insight was that the same machinery exists in us. When we are threatened, the body floods with an enormous charge of survival energy — the famous fight-or-flight mobilisation, a readiness to run or strike that is meant to be spent in action and then discharged. We are wired, like the gazelle, to shake it off afterwards. But somewhere along the way humans got socialised out of the shaking. We learn early that trembling is embarrassing, that falling apart is unseemly, that we should pull ourselves together and carry on. So the charge doesn't discharge. It gets held. The survival energy, mobilised and then forbidden its release, stays locked in the tissues and the nervous system — frozen mid-gesture, like a film paused on the frame where the runner is just beginning to flee.

Trauma, in this view, is not really the event. It is the undischarged charge left behind by the event — energy that never completed its arc. And this reframing turns out to matter enormously for neglect, though you have to turn it slightly to see why.

The Threat of Nothing in Particular

A child does not need a cheetah to flood with survival energy. A child needs only to be in distress — frightened, overwhelmed, achingly lonely — and to find that no one is coming. For a small human, whose survival genuinely depends on a caregiver's attention, the absence of a responsive adult is not a minor disappointment. It registers, at the level of the nervous system, as danger. The infant brain has no category for "my emotional needs are being underprioritised." It has only alarm, and the deep biological expectation that alarm will summon help.

When the help reliably does not come — not through cruelty, just through a parent who is checked out, or overwhelmed, or simply never learned to attune to a child's inner life — the system does something clever and costly. It stops mobilising. There is no point flooding with the energy to cry out if crying out changes nothing; the more ancient response to inescapable threat is not fight or flight but freeze, a kind of protective shutdown. The child goes quiet. Manages alone. Becomes, as so many neglected children do, remarkably self-sufficient and low-maintenance, the "easy" kid who never needed much.

And that frozen, shut-down state — held not for a single afternoon but as the default setting of a whole childhood — becomes wired into the body. This is why neglect is, paradoxically, so well suited to a body-based approach despite leaving no story. The wound was never verbal in the first place. It was laid down before language, in the pre-verbal grammar of a nervous system learning whether the world is safe. You cannot talk your way back to a place that words never reached.

Which brings us to the deepest and strangest piece of the puzzle.

The People Who Cannot Feel Their Feelings

Ask an emotionally neglected adult how they feel and you will often get one of two answers: "fine," or a long, baffled pause. This is not evasion. For a significant proportion of people who grew up this way, the honest answer is that they genuinely do not know. There is a clinical name for this: alexithymia, literally "no words for feelings." But the phrase undersells it, because the problem usually runs deeper than vocabulary. It is not just that they can't name the feeling. Often they can't detect it at all.

To see why, you need one more concept, and it is the quiet hero of this whole field: interoception. We are all taught about the five senses pointed outward at the world — sight, hearing, touch, taste, smell. Interoception is the sense pointed inward: the body's perception of its own internal state. The tightness in your chest. The hollow drop in your stomach. The heat rising in your face, the clench in your jaw, the flutter that tells you, before any thought arrives, that you are nervous. Emotions, it turns out, are not abstract mental events floating free of the flesh. They are very largely the brain's reading of these bodily signals. Fear more or less is the racing heart and the cold hands, interpreted. You feel your feelings, in the most literal sense, through your body.

And interoception is not innate in any finished form. It is learned, and it is learned relationally, in childhood. A baby cries; an attuned parent says, "Oh, you're hungry," or "You're so tired, aren't you," and feeds or soothes accordingly. Over thousands of such moments, the child's raw, undifferentiated bodily sensations get matched to words and met with the right response. The fluttery-empty feeling gets reliably called hunger and answered with food. The hot, jagged feeling gets called anger and held safely. This is how a human learns to read its own interior — by having a more experienced reader narrate it back, again and again, until the wiring takes.

For the emotionally neglected child, that narrator was absent. The sensations arrived but were never named, never reflected, never met. And so the channel itself — the link between bodily sensation and conscious feeling — was never fully built. The result is an adult who is, in a strange and lonely way, a stranger inside their own skin. They may live almost entirely in their heads, intellectually fluent, able to analyse their own psychology with impressive precision, while the body below the neck stays mute and far away. They have, in effect, gone numb to the very signals that feelings are made of.

This is the thing that ordinary talk therapy, for all its power, tends to miss. Cognitive approaches like CBT are superb at working with thoughts — at catching the distorted belief, challenging the harsh inner voice, reframing the catastrophic prediction. But they operate on the top floor of the building, in the realm of language and conscious reasoning. The neglect wound is in the basement, in a pre-verbal layer of sensation and physiological state that was damaged before the top floor was even built. You can do excellent cognitive work with someone and still leave that basement entirely untouched. They will understand their neglect beautifully and feel exactly as numb as before.

Somatic work is not, then, simply a gentler or more fashionable alternative route to the same destination. It is access to a different and prior floor of the building — one the cognitive lift doesn't reach.

Going In Slowly: Titration and Pendulation

So how do you work directly with a frozen, shut-down body without simply overwhelming it? Because that is the obvious danger. If a nervous system has spent decades in protective numbness, flinging open the doors and inviting the full force of old terror to flood in would not heal it — it would re-traumatise it, confirming all over again that the inner world is unbearable and best avoided. The whole art lies in approaching the charge gently enough that the system can actually process it.

Levine borrowed two words from chemistry and physics to describe how. The first is titration. In a chemistry lab, when you need to combine two substances that would react violently if simply poured together, you add one to the other a single drop at a time, letting each tiny increment react and settle before the next. Somatic work treats traumatic activation the same way. Rather than diving into the worst of it, the therapist helps the person touch the edge of a difficult sensation in the smallest possible dose — a flicker of tightness, a moment of unease — and then stops, letting the nervous system process that droplet before going any further. The frozen charge is metabolised gradually, in amounts small enough that the system never floods.

The second word is pendulation, from the swing of a pendulum. Here the work deliberately oscillates back and forth: a little contact with the difficult, activated sensation, and then a deliberate return to a place of settledness and safety in the body — a part that feels calm, or neutral, or even just the solid pressure of the chair beneath you. Toward the charge, then back to safety. Toward, then back. This rhythm does something important. It teaches a nervous system that has only ever known being stuck — frozen in shutdown, or flooded with alarm — that states are not permanent, that you can go toward something hard and also come back. Each swing widens what therapists call the window of tolerance: the band of arousal within which a person can stay present and functional rather than either flooding into panic or collapsing into numbness. Slowly, drop by drop, swing by swing, the system relearns that it can hold more than it thought.

Notice how different this is from telling a story. There is no narrative being constructed, no insight being chased. The work happens almost entirely in the present tense of the body: What do you notice right now? Where do you feel that? What happens if you stay with it a moment? The "felt sense" — a term the field borrowed from the philosopher and psychotherapist Eugene Gendlin — is exactly this: the vague, bodily, not-yet-verbal knowing that lives beneath our articulate thoughts, the murky sensation in the gut that knows something before the mind can say what. For someone whose interoception was never developed, simply learning to notice the felt sense at all — to register that there is anything happening in the body, and to stay with it long enough for it to clarify — is not a warm-up to the real work. It is the real work.

What Healing Actually Looks Like

It would be natural to expect the goal of all this to be some dramatic release — the human equivalent of the gazelle's great shaking discharge, a catharsis that finally lets the frozen energy out. Sometimes, in fact, something like that does happen; people in somatic work do sometimes tremble, or feel waves of heat or spontaneous movement as a long-held charge completes its interrupted arc. But for neglect specifically, the real destination is quieter, and easy to miss precisely because it is so undramatic.

What changes is not that a buried memory is finally unearthed, or that a great catharsis purges the system, or even that the person arrives at some brilliant new insight about their childhood. What changes is that the body, for perhaps the first time, begins to feel like a safe place to live. The chronic background hum of low-grade threat starts to quiet. Sensations that were once a frightening foreign language start to become legible — that hollow feeling is sadness; this tightness is fear; that warmth is something good. The stranger inside the skin slowly becomes an inhabitant. A person who spent decades hovering somewhere above their own neck begins, gently, to move back in.

There is a particular kind of poignancy here, and it is worth ending on. The emotionally neglected child learned, very early and very reasonably, that their inner world was not worth anyone's attention — including, eventually, their own. They became experts at not noticing themselves. The deepest thing body-based work offers is not the discharge of old terror but the slow undoing of that lesson: the radical, almost subversive act of turning attention inward and finding that what's there is worth attending to. It is a kind of belated answer to a question the child asked silently a thousand times and never heard answered — is anyone noticing what's happening in here? The answer, arriving decades late and from an unexpected direction, is finally yes. Someone is. And, most importantly, it turns out to be you.

That may be the truest reason the body has to be the way in. The wound was never that something bad was said or done. The wound was that no one was paying the right kind of attention. And you cannot fix that with better words about the past. You can only fix it by learning, at last, to pay that attention now — to the one place it was always meant to be paid, and never was.
