Whoever Saves Last

On Wikipedia, two people can open the same article at the same moment. One is fixing a typo in the second paragraph; the other, on the far side of the world and with no idea the first person exists, is adding a citation to the fourth. Both type. Both click save. Nothing stops them from doing this at exactly the same instant — there's no queue, no "please wait, someone else is editing" message, no lock on the page. And yet the second save doesn't quietly erase the first one. Instead, the wiki notices that the page has changed since this editor last loaded it, stops, and asks a human to sort out the difference. It happens millions of times a day, almost always invisibly, because almost always there's no actual conflict to resolve — the two edits touched different paragraphs and merge cleanly. Only rarely does a human have to step in.

That quiet mechanism — let people work as if no one else is around, and only check for trouble at the very last moment — has a name: optimistic locking. It's one of two opposite ways to answer a question every piece of software that lets more than one person touch the same data has to answer: what do we do when two people might want to change the same thing at once?

**Two bets on the same unknown**

Imagine a parking garage with a choice to make about how to run its entrance. Option one: put a barrier arm at the gate. Every car stops, takes a ticket, and waits for the arm to lift before entering. Only one car moves at a time. This guarantees, with total certainty, that two cars will never scrape doors trying to back into the same space — because only one car is ever inside the system to begin with. Call this the paranoid bet: assume collisions are likely, so prevent them structurally, even if it means everyone waits, even when the garage is nearly empty.

Option two: no arm at all. Cars just drive in. Only when a driver actually tries to pull into a specific space does anything check whether that space is free — a sensor, say, that glances at the spot right as the car noses toward it. Ninety-nine times out of a hundred the spot is exactly as free as it looked from a distance, and the driver parks without ever having paused. Only on the rare, genuinely crowded day do two cars aim for the same spot at once, and one of them has to circle the block and try again. Call this the confident bet: assume collisions are rare, let everyone move freely, and pay the cost of checking — and occasionally redoing a small amount of work — only at the very end, only when it's actually needed.

The first policy is pessimistic locking. In database terms it's the `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` statement: grab an exclusive lock on a row the moment you start working with it, and hold that lock until you're done, forcing anyone else who wants the same row to wait in line. The second is optimistic locking, and it's what Wikipedia is doing, what most modern web and mobile applications are doing, and what most large-scale distributed databases lean on by default. The interesting thing is not that one policy is "better" than the other — it's that which one is right depends entirely on a number neither policy can see in advance: how often, in this particular system, do two people actually reach for the same thing at the same time?

**Where the idea came from**

The formal version of this idea dates to a specific 1981 paper by H.T. Kung and John T. Robinson, "On Optimistic Methods for Concurrency Control," published in ACM Transactions on Database Systems. At the time, the dominant way databases handled simultaneous access was some flavor of locking — hold a lock, block other transactions, release it when you're finished. Kung and Robinson noticed something that sounds almost too simple in hindsight: in a lot of real workloads, most transactions never actually touch the same data as each other. If that's true, then a system that locks defensively on every single transaction is paying a constant tax — the overhead of acquiring and releasing locks, and the delay of transactions queuing behind one another — to prevent a problem that, most of the time, was never going to happen anyway.

Their proposal was to skip the lock entirely. Let every transaction run freely, as if it had the database to itself. Only at the moment it's ready to commit does the system do a validation check: did anything this transaction read or is about to write get changed by someone else in the meantime? If not, commit and move on — no waiting was ever required. If so, throw the transaction out and make it retry, this time working from up-to-date information. The cost of a collision is paid only by the transactions that actually collide, not spread as a tax across every transaction that runs.

**The trick underneath the trick**

Strip away the database theory and the mechanism that makes this work in practice is almost embarrassingly simple, and it's worth sitting with because the same three-step pattern shows up everywhere optimistic locking is used, from Wikipedia to shopping carts to distributed databases running across continents.

Every row of data gets a hidden extra column: a version number, or sometimes a timestamp. It might be called `version`, `etag`, `_rev`, or `lock_version` depending on the system, but it's doing the same job everywhere: it's a stamp that says "this is the Nth time this row has been changed."

Reading a row never touches that stamp — you just read the data along with whatever version number happens to be attached right now, and you carry that number along with you while you think, edit, or compute.

Writing back is where the check happens, and it happens as a single atomic instruction, something like: "update this row, but only if its version number still equals the one I originally read — and while you're at it, bump the version number by one." If nobody else touched the row in between, the version still matches, the write succeeds, and the version ticks forward to mark that a change happened. If someone else got there first, the version has already moved — it's a step ahead of what you're holding — and your conditional update simply fails to match any row. No exception needs to be thrown by some separate lock-checking process; the failure is baked directly into the fact that zero rows matched the update condition. The application catches that "zero rows affected" result, understands it means a collision occurred, and re-fetches the current version of the data so the user (or the retry logic) can try again with fresh information.

This is exactly what's happening under Wikipedia's hood, dressed in slightly different clothes: the page carries a revision ID, and when you submit an edit, the software checks whether the revision ID you started from still matches the page's current revision ID. If it doesn't, your edit becomes an "edit conflict," and — because merging prose automatically is hard — a human is shown both versions and asked to reconcile them by hand. It's also what a REST API is doing when it uses ETags and `If-Match` headers: the client sends back the version tag it received, the server rejects the update if that tag is stale, and the client gets a 409 Conflict instead of silently clobbering someone else's change. It's what an ORM like Hibernate is doing with its `@Version` annotation on a database column, and what many NoSQL databases do with conditional writes — DynamoDB, for instance, lets you say "put this item, but only if its current version attribute equals X," which is the exact same trick expressed as an API call instead of SQL.

**Why the bet pays off almost everywhere**

The reason optimistic locking has become the quiet default across so much of modern software — web apps, mobile apps, most distributed systems — is that the confident bet turns out to be correct far more often than intuition suggests. Consider a typical web application: thousands of users, each editing their own profile, their own document, their own shopping cart. The chance that user A and user B are simultaneously trying to update the exact same row, at the exact same moment, is vanishingly small, because the data is naturally partitioned by user. In that world, pessimistic locking is pure overhead: it makes every single request pay a locking cost to guard against a collision that will essentially never occur for that particular piece of data. Optimistic locking lets every reader move at full speed and only pays a retry cost on the rare occasion two writers genuinely collide — precisely mirroring the nearly-empty parking garage that never needed a barrier arm in the first place.

There's a second reason optimistic locking fits distributed systems specifically: a lock held by pessimistic locking is a lock held across the network, potentially across data centers on different continents, for as long as some client is slow to finish its work. If that client crashes, goes to lunch, or just has a slow connection, the lock can sit there blocking everyone else indefinitely, or until some timeout mechanism kicks in to clean up the mess. Optimistic locking never has this failure mode, because it never holds anything open in the first place — there's no lock to leak, no client to wait on, no timeout to configure. It converts a coordination problem (who has the lock right now, and are they still alive?) into a much simpler, self-contained check performed entirely at write time.

**Where the bet collapses**

But a bet, by definition, can lose — and optimistic locking loses in exactly the situation its logic predicts: high contention on the same narrow piece of data. Picture a flash sale with one thousand units of a wildly popular item, ten thousand people simultaneously hammering the "buy" button. Every one of those ten thousand requests wants to read the current inventory count and write back a decremented version. Under optimistic locking, the first request to write wins, bumps the version number, and every other request that had already read the now-stale count fails its version check and has to retry. But the retries aren't hitting an empty garage anymore — they're hitting the same jammed intersection as everyone else, so a large fraction of the retries fail too, and have to retry again. This is the garage on its single most crowded day of the year, the exact scenario the barrier arm was built for: with genuine, sustained contention on one narrow resource, optimistic locking's core assumption — that collisions are rare — simply isn't true anymore, and the system starts paying repeatedly for the same collision instead of paying once for a queue.

This failure mode has a name that captures how bad it can get: a retry storm. Each failed write isn't free — if the logic being retried involves anything beyond a trivial database update (charging a payment method, calling an external inventory API, sending a confirmation), each retry re-runs that expensive work too. Under enough contention, a system can spend more total effort retrying and re-validating than it would ever have spent simply queuing requests behind a lock in the first place. This is precisely why high-traffic checkout systems for scarce inventory often reach for pessimistic locking, or a hybrid — queue-based serialization, or a dedicated inventory-reservation service — specifically for that one narrow hot path, while leaving everything else in the application running on the optimistic default. The choice isn't ideological; it's a straightforward read of which bet the traffic pattern actually supports.

**The bet changes as the crowd changes**

What makes this framing useful beyond the mechanics is that it reorients the question engineers actually need to ask. The naive version of the question is "should we use optimistic or pessimistic locking?" — as if it were a fixed property of a system, chosen once at design time and left alone. The better version of the question is "how confident are we that two actors will rarely reach for this specific piece of data at the same moment — and is that confidence still justified as the system grows?" A social media app's comment-editing feature can run optimistic locking forever, because two people are essentially never editing the exact same comment at once. But the same app's "like counter" on a viral post, hit by tens of thousands of near-simultaneous writes, behaves nothing like the comment field — the contention profile has completely changed even though the underlying database technology hasn't.

That's really what the parking garage image is for: not a cute illustration to make locking sound friendlier, but a genuine diagnostic. Before building the barrier arm, ask how full the garage actually gets. Most software, most of the time, is closer to empty than anyone assumes — which is exactly why the confident bet, quietly checking a version number instead of standing guard at the door, has become the unremarked-upon default running underneath so much of what we build. It doesn't announce itself the way a lock does. It just lets everyone in, and only speaks up on the rare afternoon when two cars actually want the same spot.
